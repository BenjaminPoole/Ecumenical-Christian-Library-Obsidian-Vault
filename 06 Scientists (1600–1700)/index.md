# The Theology of the Scientists

The religious writings of the men who made the scientific revolution — 11 authors,
57 works, 584 notes, from Bacon's *Confession of Faith* to Newton on the
prophecies of Daniel. The seventeenth century did not divide these men into a scientific
half and a devout half, and neither did they: Boyle published more theology than
chemistry, Newton left more words on prophecy than on optics, and Ray's natural history
and his natural theology are the same enquiry under two names. The section is here because
the argument between faith and science is usually conducted in ignorance of what the
scientists themselves actually wrote.

Two kinds of source. 34 of the works are **hand-keyed transcriptions of the first
editions** from the [Text Creation Partnership](https://textcreationpartnership.org),
which had them typed from the page images and proofread rather than scanned, and released
them under CC0; those keep the spelling, capitals and italics of their own century, and
mark with […] the few words the transcribers could not read. The other twenty-three come from
[Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org), in the editions named on each work's page.
Editors' introductions are not included — provenance belongs here and in the frontmatter,
not in the text.

Everything is in the public domain with **one exception**, which is flagged on its own
page: Leibniz's *Theodicy* exists in English only in E. M. Huggard's translation of 1951,
which Project Gutenberg publishes as public domain in the United States and which may
still be in copyright elsewhere.

Authors are in order of birth, and each author's works in the order the vault holds them.


## Francis Bacon

Francis Bacon (1561–1626), Lord Chancellor and the man who argued English natural philosophy out of Aristotle and into experiment. He held the study of nature and the study of Scripture to be two books of the same author, and wrote privately on both all his life.

Author index: **[[Francis Bacon|Francis Bacon]]**

- **[[A Confession of Faith|A Confession of Faith]]** — Bacon's own creed, written in private and printed in 1641, fifteen years after his death, as *penned by an orthodox man of the reformed religion*. *(1 note)*
- **[[A Letter to Mr. Matthew, Imprisoned for Religion|A Letter to Mr. Matthew, Imprisoned for Religion]]** — Bacon's letter to his friend Tobie Matthew, imprisoned in 1607 for his conversion to Rome — a Protestant's letter of comfort to a Catholic prisoner, and the only one of his many letters to Matthew about religion. *(1 note)*
- **[[An Advertisement Touching an Holy War|An Advertisement Touching an Holy War]]** — An unfinished dialogue, written in 1622 after Bacon's fall, on whether a war made for religion can be lawful. *(2 notes)*
- **[[An Advertisement Touching the Controversies of the Church of England|An Advertisement Touching the Controversies of the Church of England]]** — Bacon's earliest surviving work, written at twenty-eight during the Martin Marprelate quarrel and printed seventy years later as *True Peace*. *(1 note)*
- **[[Certain Considerations Touching the Church of England|Certain Considerations Touching the Church of England]]** — Written for James I in the first year of the reign, when the Hampton Court conference had just closed. *(7 notes)*
- **[[Five Essays on Religion|Five Essays on Religion]]** — The five of Bacon's fifty-eight *Essays or Counsels* whose subject is religion, gathered here under a title of the vault's own, from the final edition of 1625: on truth, where Pilate jests and would not stay for an answer; on death; on the unity of the church, and when zeal becomes a scandal; on atheism, where *a… *(5 notes)*
- **[[New Atlantis|New Atlantis]]** — Bacon's unfinished fable of Bensalem, an island in the South Sea converted to Christianity by a miracle twenty years after the Ascension and governed by Salomon's House, a college of natural philosophers who open their work with prayer and count their discoveries as praise. *(2 notes)*
- **[[Religious Meditations|Religious Meditations]]** — The *Meditationes Sacrae*, twelve short Latin meditations on texts of Scripture that Bacon printed with the first edition of his *Essays* in 1597, in the English translation substituted for them in the reprint of the next year: on the works of God and man, the miracles of Christ, charity to enemies, earthly hope… *(1 note)*
- **[[The Characters of a Believing Christian|The Characters of a Believing Christian]]** — Three short religious pieces printed together at the end of Bacon's *Remaines*: the *Characters*, which sets out the Christian life as a string of paradoxes; a second and fuller recension of the *Confession of the Faith*; and the prayer Bacon wrote after his fall. *(4 notes)*
- **[[The Translation of Certain Psalms into English Verse|The Translation of Certain Psalms into English Verse]]** — Seven psalms put into English verse in the last year of Bacon's life, during a long illness, and dedicated to George Herbert — *in respect of our ancient and private acquaintance, and in respect of your gifts, being not only a great wit, but a great poet.* The only verse Bacon published. *(7 notes)*
- **[[Two Prayers|Two Prayers]]** — The Student's Prayer and the Writer's Prayer, which Bacon's chaplain William Rawley preserved and Archbishop Tenison printed: that the opening of the gates of sense may not close the mind to the divine mysteries, and that the work of the hands may return to God's glory. *(1 note)*

## Galileo Galilei

Galileo Galilei (1564–1642), who found the moons of Jupiter and the phases of Venus, and then had to answer for what they meant for the plain sense of Scripture.

Author index: **[[Galileo Galilei|Galileo Galilei]]**

- **[[Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina|Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina]]** — Galileo's defence of the Copernican opinion against the charge that it contradicts Scripture, written to Christina of Lorraine, mother of the Grand Duke of Tuscany, in 1615 — the year before the Congregation of the Index condemned the doctrine. *(1 note)*

## Johannes Kepler

Johannes Kepler (1571–1630), Imperial Mathematician, who found the three laws of planetary motion and made the same case about Scripture six years before Galileo.

Author index: **[[Johannes Kepler|Johannes Kepler]]**

- **[[An Abstract of the Introduction upon Mars|An Abstract of the Introduction upon Mars]]** — The theological part of Kepler's preface to the *Astronomia Nova* of 1609, abridged and put into English by Thomas Salusbury and printed with Galileo's letter to the Grand Duchess. *(1 note)*

## René Descartes

René Descartes (1596–1650), who invented coordinate geometry and set out to rebuild knowledge from what cannot be doubted — and dedicated the result to the Sorbonne's faculty of theology.

Author index: **[[René Descartes|René Descartes]]**

- **[[A Discourse of a Method|A Discourse of a Method]]** — The *Discours de la méthode* of 1637, in the English of 1649: the story of how Descartes came to doubt everything he had been taught and what he found he could not doubt. *(8 notes)*
- **[[Six Metaphysical Meditations|Six Metaphysical Meditations]]** — The *Meditationes de Prima Philosophia* of 1641, dedicated to the Faculty of Theology of the Sorbonne and offered to it as a proof, by natural reason alone, that there is a God and that the mind is distinct from the body. *(9 notes)*

## Thomas Browne

Sir Thomas Browne (1605–1682), physician of Norwich, who wrote down his own religion for himself and produced one of the great books of English prose.

Author index: **[[Thomas Browne|Thomas Browne]]**

- **[[A Letter to a Friend|A Letter to a Friend]]** — *Upon occasion of the death of his intimate friend* — a physician's account of a patient's dying, written to the friend who was not there, and ending in a string of counsels on how to live that Browne later worked up into *Christian Morals*. *(1 note)*
- **[[Christian Morals|Christian Morals]]** — Browne's last work, found among his papers and printed in 1716 by John Jeffery, Archdeacon of Norwich. *(4 notes)*
- **[[Hydriotaphia, Urn-Burial|Hydriotaphia, Urn-Burial]]** — Occasioned by some urns dug up in a Norfolk field, and turning, by way of the burial customs of every nation Browne had read of, into a meditation on death, oblivion and the resurrection of the body. *(6 notes)*
- **[[Religio Medici|Religio Medici]]** — *The religion of a physician* — written about 1635 for Browne's own use, circulated in manuscript, and printed without his leave in 1642, which forced the authorised edition of the next year. *(3 notes)*

## John Wilkins

John Wilkins (1614–1672), Bishop of Chester and first secretary of the Royal Society, in whose rooms at Oxford the Society began.

Author index: **[[John Wilkins|John Wilkins]]**

- **[[A Discourse Concerning a New World and Another Planet|A Discourse Concerning a New World and Another Planet]]** — Two books published together in 1640: *The Discovery of a New World*, arguing that the moon may be a habitable world, and *A Discourse Concerning a New Planet*, arguing that the earth is one of the planets. *(26 notes)*
- **[[A Discourse Concerning the Beauty of Providence|A Discourse Concerning the Beauty of Providence]]** — Printed in the year of the king's execution and offered as *very seasonable to quiet and support the heart in these times of public confusion*. *(2 notes)*
- **[[A Discourse Concerning the Gift of Prayer|A Discourse Concerning the Gift of Prayer]]** — What the gift of prayer is, why it is ascribed to the Spirit, and how far it may be attained by industry — written in the middle of the quarrel over set forms and free prayer, and answering it by teaching the thing itself. *(31 notes)*
- **[[Ecclesiastes, or a Discourse Concerning the Gift of Preaching|Ecclesiastes, or a Discourse Concerning the Gift of Preaching]]** — Wilkins's handbook for preachers, first printed in 1646 and enlarged in later editions: the method of a sermon, its matter, and its expression, with a long catalogue of the commentators on every book of Scripture and the authors on every head of divinity, which is what the book was chiefly used for. *(2 notes)*
- **[[Of the Principles and Duties of Natural Religion|Of the Principles and Duties of Natural Religion]]** — Left unfinished at Wilkins's death in 1672 and completed for the press by John Tillotson. *(28 notes)*
- **[[Sermons Preached Before the King at White-Hall|Sermons Preached Before the King at White-Hall]]** — Three sermons Wilkins preached before Charles II in the last years of his life, on the length of days in Wisdom's right hand, on the whole duty of man, and on the judgement of every secret thing. *(5 notes)*
- **[[Sermons Preached upon Several Occasions|Sermons Preached upon Several Occasions]]** — Fifteen sermons found among Wilkins's papers and published ten years after his death: on the recompence of reward, on the kingdom of God as righteousness and peace, on adorning the doctrine of God, on wisdom and its companions, on moderation, and two on leaving vengeance to God. *(16 notes)*

## Blaise Pascal

Blaise Pascal (1623–1662), mathematician, physicist, and after the night of fire in 1654 the defender of Port-Royal and the author of the unfinished apology.

- **[[Pensées|Pensées]]** — The notes for an apology for the Christian religion that Pascal did not live to write, found after his death in 1662 in bundles pinned together and printed by Port-Royal eight years later. *(14 notes)*
- **[[Blaise Pascal/The Memorial|The Memorial]]** — The record of the night of 23 November 1654, written on a scrap of paper and copied on to parchment, which Pascal kept sewn into the lining of his coat for the rest of his life and which a servant found there after his death. *(1 note)*

## Robert Boyle

Robert Boyle (1627–1691), founding fellow of the Royal Society and the father of chemistry, who wrote as much theology as natural philosophy and endowed a lecture to go on arguing after his death.

Author index: **[[Robert Boyle|Robert Boyle]]**

- **[[A Discourse of Things Above Reason|A Discourse of Things Above Reason]]** — A dialogue between four speakers on whether a philosopher may admit that some things are above reason, and on the difference between a doctrine reason cannot reach and one it can refute. *(9 notes)*
- **[[A Disquisition About the Final Causes of Natural Things|A Disquisition About the Final Causes of Natural Things]]** — Whether, and with what caution, a naturalist may argue from the use of a thing to the end it was made for. *(27 notes)*
- **[[A Free Discourse Against Customary Swearing|A Free Discourse Against Customary Swearing]]** — Written in Boyle's twenties and published in the year of his death by John Williams. *(26 notes)*
- **[[A Free Enquiry into the Vulgarly Received Notion of Nature|A Free Enquiry into the Vulgarly Received Notion of Nature]]** — Boyle's attack on the word *Nature* itself, as it was used by the Aristotelians: a wise and provident being standing between God and the world, which abhors a vacuum and does nothing in vain. *(10 notes)*
- **[[Occasional Reflections upon Several Subjects|Occasional Reflections upon Several Subjects]]** — Meditations made out of whatever happened to be in front of him, with the discourse defending the practice printed before them. *(97 notes)*
- **[[Of the High Veneration Man's Intellect Owes to God|Of the High Veneration Man's Intellect Owes to God]]** — On the distance between what God is and what a mind can hold of him, argued from the two attributes a natural philosopher meets first: his wisdom and his power. *(3 notes)*
- **[[Seraphic Love|Seraphic Love]]** — Boyle's first published book, written at twenty-one as a letter to a friend disappointed in love, and the work he was best known for in his own lifetime — it went through six editions. *(4 notes)*
- **[[Some Considerations Touching the Style of the Holy Scriptures|Some Considerations Touching the Style of the Holy Scriptures]]** — Boyle's defence of the Bible's style, written in his twenties as part of a longer essay on the Scriptures and addressed to a friend he calls Theophilus. *(11 notes)*
- **[[Some Considerations Touching the Usefulness of Experimental Natural Philosophy|Some Considerations Touching the Usefulness of Experimental Natural Philosophy]]** — The first part of the *Usefulness* — five essays addressed to *Pyrophilus* on the use of natural philosophy to the mind of man: that the study of nature leads to the knowledge and worship of its Author, that it is a kind of religious service, and, in the long digression of the fourth essay, against those who would… *(7 notes)*
- **[[The Christian Virtuoso|The Christian Virtuoso]]** — Boyle's answer to the charge that experimental philosophy makes men irreligious: that it does the opposite, since the man who has looked hardest at the contrivance of things is the last to think them accidental. *(14 notes)*
- **[[The Excellency of Theology|The Excellency of Theology]]** — Written in 1665 during the plague and printed nine years later. *(15 notes)*
- **[[The Martyrdom of Theodora and of Didymus|The Martyrdom of Theodora and of Didymus]]** — Written in Boyle's early twenties and published forty years later at the urging of friends: the martyrdom under Diocletian of Theodora of Antioch, sentenced to a brothel for refusing to sacrifice, and of the Christian soldier Didymus who changed clothes with her to get her out and died in her place. *(13 notes)*
- **[[The Reconcileableness of Reason and Religion|The Reconcileableness of Reason and Religion]]** — Published under the initials T.E., *a lay-man*. *(14 notes)*

## John Ray

John Ray (1627–1705), the father of English natural history, whose divinity lectures at Trinity became the founding book of English natural theology.

Author index: **[[John Ray|John Ray]]**

- **[[A Persuasive to a Holy Life|A Persuasive to a Holy Life]]** — Ray's last book, and the only one of his that is straightforwardly practical divinity: that a holy life is also the happy one, argued through every good men actually pursue — health, safety, riches, pleasure, honour, friends — and then through the happiness of the inward man and of the life to come. *(14 notes)*
- **[[The Wisdom of God Manifested in the Works of the Creation|The Wisdom of God Manifested in the Works of the Creation]]** — The book that made natural theology an English habit of mind, grown out of Ray's divinity lectures in the chapel of Trinity College, Cambridge. *(12 notes)*
- **[[Three Physico-Theological Discourses|Three Physico-Theological Discourses]]** — Three discourses on the earth's own history: the creation of the world, the general deluge — with the question of what fossils are, which Ray was among the first to answer correctly — and the dissolution of the world by fire. *(26 notes)*

## Isaac Newton

Isaac Newton (1642–1727), who gave the world one mathematics for the apple and the moon, and spent as long again on the prophets.

Author index: **[[Isaac Newton|Isaac Newton]]**

- **[[Four Letters to Dr Bentley|Four Letters to Dr Bentley]]** — Newton's answers to Richard Bentley, who was preparing the first Boyle Lectures, *A Confutation of Atheism*, and wrote to ask how far the *Principia* could be used in them. *(4 notes)*
- **[[Observations upon the Prophecies of Daniel and the Apocalypse of St John|Observations upon the Prophecies of Daniel and the Apocalypse of St John]]** — Newton spent more of his life on prophecy and chronology than on optics, and left this unpublished at his death; his nephew Benjamin Smith saw it through the press in 1733. *(18 notes)*
- **[[Queries 28 and 31 of the Opticks|Queries 28 and 31 of the Opticks]]** — The two of the thirty-one questions closing the *Opticks* in which Newton follows natural philosophy to its end in God. *(2 notes)*
- **[[The Chronology of Ancient Kingdoms Amended|The Chronology of Ancient Kingdoms Amended]]** — Published the year after Newton's death by John Conduitt, his nephew by marriage. *(10 notes)*

## Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716), co-inventor of the calculus, who coined the word *theodicy* and wrote the book.

Author index: **[[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz|Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz]]**

- **[[Discourse on Metaphysics|Discourse on Metaphysics]]** — Leibniz's first full statement of his system, sent in outline to Arnauld in 1686: that God acts in the most perfect way, that each substance expresses the whole universe, and that minds are citizens of the City of God. *(1 note)*
- **[[The Monadology|The Monadology]]** — The ninety numbered propositions in which Leibniz summed up his philosophy two years before his death, ending with God as the monarch of the City of spirits. *(1 note)*
- **[[The Principles of Nature and of Grace|The Principles of Nature and of Grace]]** — The companion to the *Monadology*, written the same year: from the Monads through the principle of sufficient reason to God, the love of God, and the perpetual progress of the blessed. *(1 note)*
- **[[Theodicy|Theodicy]]** — The only book Leibniz published in his lifetime, written against Pierre Bayle's argument that the evil in the world cannot be reconciled with a God at once good and almighty. *(9 notes)*

## Missing Works

**Kepler's own words in English are almost all missing.** The *Astronomia Nova*, the
*Harmonices Mundi* with its closing prayer, and the *Mysterium Cosmographicum* have no
public-domain English translation; the only piece here is Salusbury's 1661 abridgement of
one preface. Wallis's translation of the *Harmonies of the World* (1939) and the modern
Donahue are both in copyright.

**Christiaan Huygens (1629–1695)** wrote *Cosmotheoros*, printed in English in 1698 as
*The Celestial Worlds Discover'd* — an argument from the plurality of worlds to the
greatness of their maker. It is public domain and reachable, and is the obvious next
addition.

**Nicolas Steno (1638–1686)** founded stratigraphy, converted to Rome in 1667 and died a
bishop; his devotional and controversial works are in Latin and Danish and have no
public-domain English.

**The wider Royal Society circle** wrote a great deal of theology that is in the public
domain and hand-keyed in the Text Creation Partnership, and none of it is here, because
the section was scoped to the ten names the century is usually remembered by:

**Boyle's *Some Considerations Touching the Style of the Holy Scriptures*** (1661) is the
one work of his that is named in every account of his theology and is not here: it is not
among the Text Creation Partnership's transcriptions, and would have to come from a scan
of Birch's *Works* (1772) by way of OCR.

**Robert Hooke, Edmond Halley and Antonie van Leeuwenhoek** left no theological writings
to add.
