# Friedrich Schleiermacher

Friedrich Daniel Ernst Schleiermacher (1768–1834), raised among the Moravian Brethren, preacher at the Charité hospital in Berlin and then at the Dreifaltigkeitskirche, professor at Halle and a founder of the University of Berlin. Called the father of modern liberal theology: religion, he argued, is neither knowing nor doing but feeling — in *The Christian Faith*, "the feeling of absolute dependence" — and doctrine is the church's account of that feeling as Christ has shaped it. He was also the founder of modern hermeneutics, Plato's translator, and a preacher every Sunday of his working life.

## Works (3)

- **[[On Religion|On Religion]]** — Five speeches addressed to the educated Romantics of Berlin who had turned their backs on religion: religion is neither metaphysics nor morals but "a sense and taste for the Infinite". The book from which Protestant liberal theology is usually dated. Oman translated the third edition (1821) with Schleiermacher's later explanations, and gives the changes from the first edition in an appendix. (7 notes)
- **[[Selected Sermons|Selected Sermons]]** — Twenty-seven sermons from Schleiermacher's ministry at Halle and at the Dreifaltigkeitskirche in Berlin, with a biographical sketch by the translator. The third was preached in 1813, in the war of liberation against Napoleon. (28 notes)
- **[[The Christian Faith|The Christian Faith]]** — The great systematic theology of the father of modern liberal theology, in which doctrine is the church's account of the Christian's God-consciousness, "the feeling of absolute dependence", as Christ has shaped it. After an introduction on the church, on Christianity and on the method of dogmatics, the first part develops the religious self-consciousness that every Christian experience presupposes (creation, preservation, and the divine attributes of eternity, omnipresence, omnipotence and omniscience); the second part sets out its two sides, the consciousness of sin (original and actual sin, the holiness and justice of God) and the consciousness of grace (the person and work of Christ, regeneration, sanctification, election, the church, the means of grace and the last things); and a conclusion treats the divine Trinity. The book's indexes at the end are left out. (62 notes)
