# Commentary on Romans, Chapter 3

*While almost all of the works in this vault use Victorian translations, due to the English translations of this commentary being in copyright, it was machine-translated (by Claude, 2026) from the public-domain Latin. It has not been checked by a human translator.*

*Commentary on Paul's Epistle to the Romans, long printed among the works of Ambrose. Its author, whom Erasmus first called "Ambrosiaster", wrote at Rome while Damasus was bishop there (366–384). Chapter 3 of the Epistle; the verse numbers are the Benedictine editors'.*

*(Verses 1–2.) What advantage then hath the Jew? or what profit is there of circumcision? Much every way: chiefly, because that unto them were committed the oracles of God.* Though there are many things that belong to the honour and merit of the seed of Abraham, he openly mentions only this one, because it is the greatest of their praises: that they were judged worthy to receive the Law, through which they might learn to tell right from wrong; so that after this first thing the rest might be understood for what they are. But since he has shown that the witness of their birth profits the carnal Jews, that is, the unbelieving, nothing at all, he teaches that it is of great use to the believing Jews, because they are sons of Abraham — lest he should seem to have dealt hardly with all of them, the believers as well. For to them the oracles of God were committed, when they received the Law renewed for the merit of the fathers — the Law which had fallen out of use through men's sins, as though one could sin against God with impunity — and were called the people of God. Egypt, too, was struck with various plagues because of the wrongs done to them; they were fed with bread from heaven; they were a terror to all nations, as Rahab the harlot testifies; and to them, besides, Christ the Saviour was promised for their sanctification (Joshua 2:9 ff.). This is why he says that it profits the Jews much every way, because they are sons of Abraham: for they are set before the Gentiles — but those of them who believe.

*(Verse 3.) For what if some did not believe? shall their unbelief make the faith of God without effect? God forbid.* He says: because some of the Jews would not believe what God promised to the sons of Abraham, the rest of the Jews shall not be prejudged for that, so as to be called unworthy to receive what God promised to the faithful; since the promise was made in such a way as to profit those who believe. So God, not made indignant by the faithlessness of those others, that is, of the Jews, will give to the rest who believe the eternal life which He promised would come through faith in Christ: because those who did not believe made themselves unworthy without prejudice to the rest. In saying this he commends the believing Jews, since it does them no harm that many of their people would not believe.

*(Verse 4.) Yea, let God be true, but every man a liar; as it is written, That thou mightest be justified in thy sayings, and mightest overcome when thou art judged* (Psalm 51:4). To confirm what he said above — that God is faithful in His promise, and every man a liar — he adds a prophetic instance from the fiftieth psalm, in which he marked both that God is justified in His words and that man is a liar because of his unbelief. For both are read in the book of Psalms: that God is just and true, and that every man is a liar (Psalm 86:15; 116:11). He calls man a liar when he does not believe what God has promised: for in denying that God will give what He promised, he is a liar; and while he himself is a liar, he declares God a liar on many grounds when he does not believe His promises. So he calls every man a liar who distrusts the promises of God. Yet this belongs most of all to the Jews, whose case the Apostle is treating in this place: for seeing Christ, they denied that He was the one whom God had promised, and so they are declared liars. But God is true, who sent Christ as He promised; and so He overcomes when He is judged, because He will give what it is denied He will give. For when He is not believed, He is judged a liar; but when He gives what He was judged not about to give, He overcomes, showing Himself true, and him a liar who did not believe His words. For he will see in majesty the Son of God whom he denies; he will see the resurrection of the flesh as well, and will know that faithlessness has been overcome by the promise and the truth of God. For Christ too, when He was judged, overcame: for He was put to death unjustly, and made the devil guilty — whom He had already overcome by not sinning — so that, overcome a second time, the devil should lose those whom he held.

*(Verses 5–6.) But if our unrighteousness commend the righteousness of God, what shall we say? Is God unrighteous who taketh vengeance? (I speak as a man.) God forbid.* The Apostle says this following the sense of the prophet David. When David had sinned in the matter of Uriah the Hittite (2 Samuel 11:4), knowing that the promise cannot be given to sinners, he prays that the justification of God's words may overcome the judgment by which those who sin are judged (Psalm 51:4), and that God, sanctifying him, may renew him in his repentance, so as to give him what He promised to give the just. And so he added, *But if our unrighteousness commend the righteousness of God*, and so on: that is, if God is justified because we are sinners, He will be unjust if He takes vengeance. For if our unrighteousness profited Him, He would without doubt be unjust if He condemned sinners: since our unrighteousness makes God just, if He gives us, though we sin, what He promised to the holy. For though we are sinners, yet we are renewed through repentance, so that we may deserve to receive the promise no longer as sinners but as men washed clean. That, then, is not the sense of the prophet David's words when he says, *Against thee only have I sinned, and done this evil in thy sight: that thou mightest be justified when thou speakest, and overcome when thou art judged* (ibid.), which is claimed for them by the twisted interpretation of malicious men: that men's sins and evil deeds should profit God's justification, and that He should appear good through our evils, and our unrighteousness show Him righteous. Since, then, God is not unrighteous when He inflicts wrath, He is not justified by our sins: for if He were justified by our sins, He would be unrighteous if He punished them. But since He is not unrighteous when He punishes, it is plain that our unrighteousness does not commend the righteousness of God. For if it did commend it, He would not punish, because He is just. And so he added, *I speak*, he says, *as a man. God forbid*: that is, far be it that God should be called unrighteous; for that belongs to man, who, as is well known, both errs, and is deceived, and deceives. But God remains unchangeable, and cannot but love what He has made. *For then how shall God judge the world?* It is true that it would not be just for God to judge the world, if its sins profited Him: so that God should appear good when sinners obtain pardon at His nod; and if they did not sin, He would not appear just. For if they do not sin, He will not be good, having no one to forgive.

*(Verse 7.) For if the truth of God hath more abounded through my lie unto his glory; why yet am I also judged as a sinner?* It is plain that if the lie of men profits the glory of God, so that He alone appears true, those who sin are not to be called sinners: because they will seem to sin not by their own will, but by His impulse — which God forbid.

*(Verse 8.) And not rather, (as we be slanderously reported, and as some affirm that we say,) Let us do evil, that good may come?* Now he discloses on whose account he argues these things so carefully and with such reverence. For this objection was raised by perverse men, as though this were the meaning of those who preach the forgiveness of sins: that men should do evil and good should come — that is, that they should sin, so that God might appear good by forgiving them, according to what was said above. He calls this a slander, and casts it out of the meaning of the divine teaching. For faith does not teach that one ought to sin, since it preaches that God will judge; but it takes thought for those who have offended, so that, having recovered salvation, they may live under the law of God and sin no more. And so he added, *whose damnation is just*: that is, the damnation of these men is just, who, from the rivalry of envy, interpret what was said above in this way about us. For the Jews bandied this about to slander the apostolic teaching, saying that when they preach the forgiveness of sins they furnish fuel for sinning; so that men, as though secure of forgiveness, should sin readily: whereas it is well known that after receiving the faith it is perilous to sin, and this is preached to believers.

*(Verse 9.) What then? are we better than they? No, in no wise: for we have before proved both Jews and Gentiles, that they are all under sin.* That is: why do we still linger, speaking at greater length? For we have shown, giving our reasons, that Jews as well as Greeks are all guilty, and that therefore the law is kept in vain. First he showed the Greeks guilty by the law of nature, and because they would not receive the Law of Moses either; for which reason their case is the worst and the gravest. Then he showed that the Jews too are guilty, who seem to live under the law of God and claimed dignity for themselves for the fathers' merit — above all in this, that they spurned the promise made to the fathers, bringing the grace of God to nothing. To confirm this he added a prophetic instance from the thirteenth psalm, saying:

*(Verse 10.) As it is written, There is none righteous, no, not one* (Psalm 14:1 ff.). He began from unrighteousness to list their evil works, and went on to add some still worse; to make it plain that no hope is to be seen in them, unless they implore the mercy of Christ, who forgives sins.

*(Verses 11–12.) Then he added: There is none that understandeth.* It is true: for if he took pains to understand, he would not be unrighteous. *There is none that seeketh after God.* Nor is this hidden: for if he understood what was good for him, he would seek God — and not like Asa king of Judah, who after many benefits from God was so corrupted that, when he suffered a disease in his feet, he did not seek God, though a prophet was at hand. *They are all gone out of the way, they are together become unprofitable* (1 Kings 15:23). No one doubts that all who do not seek God turn aside, to seek help from vanity; and vanity is an idol; and so they become unprofitable. *There is none that doeth good, no, not one.* Because, having neglected God, they have become unprofitable, they certainly cannot do good: for, once corrupted, they go forward to worse.

*(Verse 13.) Their throat is an open sepulchre.* Given over now to evil, they wished, if it could be done, to devour the good: so that, as a sepulchre stands open to receive dead bodies, so their throat stands open against the good. *With their tongues they have used deceit.* Being so used to evil works, what they spoke was in falsehood. *The poison of asps is under their lips.* He says this because the words of such men are traps: for they speak in order to deceive; so that, as poison is poured out through the lips of a serpent, so deceit and overreaching are poured out through their lips.

*(Verse 14.) Whose mouth is full of cursing and bitterness.* It is plain that the wicked always set out curses and bitter things against the good, breaking out into abuse and disparagement of them.

*(Verse 15.) Their feet are swift to shed blood.* He said this of the slaying of the prophets, whom they killed without hesitation: slow to good, swift to murder.

*(Verse 16.) Destruction and misery are in their ways.* Since they were hurried on to doing evil, he declares their way, or their going, to be troubled and unhappy. *(Verse 17.) And the way of peace have they not known.* The way of peace is gentle and undisturbed: for every good life is tranquil, and modest acts are themselves peaceful, and by these one goes to God. Those, then, unwilling to know this, chose the way of troubles, by which one goes to hell.

*(Verse 18.) There is no fear of God before their eyes.* Since men of this kind are without sense, they have no fear of God: for *the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom*, says Solomon (Proverbs 1:7). And he did not say that these men have no fear of God, but, *There is no fear of God*, he says, *before their eyes*. For seeing such malicious works of theirs and not shuddering at them, they are said not to have the fear of God before their eyes. The prophet Jeremiah too means all these men, when he says among other things, *Then all rose up against the prophet of the Lord, wishing to kill him*; and it follows, *But all the people did not permit it* (Jeremiah 26:8, 16). He says "all", then, of the one, but the wicked; and "all" of the others, but the good. For in saying, *They are all gone out of the way*, he does not mean the whole people without exception, but this part of the people, as the prophet mentioned also says: *In which all the wicked have hidden themselves.* For there are always two peoples in one nation. This, then, is the people that the Lord rebukes under the name of Jerusalem, saying, *O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, thou that killest the prophets* (Matthew 23:37); and in another place, *An evil and adulterous generation*; and, *Generation of vipers* (Matthew 12:39, 34). The prophet Isaiah too complains of such, saying, *Ah sinful nation, a seed of evildoers!* (Isaiah 1:4). For by an evil will, while they bear such fruits, they are a most evil seed. And that, if they will, they can be converted, the Apostle's own words in another epistle prove: *And were by nature the children of wrath, even as others* (Ephesians 2:3) — we too were, once.

*(Verse 19.) Now we know that what things soever the law saith, it saith to them who are under the law.* It is plain that the Law rebukes those who first did not believe Moses their leader, nor the prophets their kinsmen, whom they persecuted and killed, nor the apostles their kinsmen according to the flesh, whose blood they shed: always ungodly and rebellious against God; so that they are condemned by the law whose authority they thought should be despised. For as there is one cause of all evils in the evil one, so also of all good things. In these men, then, the evil of all who are like them is convicted. These things serve to confound the Jews, who brought testimony from the law for themselves and their fathers.

*That every mouth may be stopped, and all the world may become guilty before God.* He says this because, the Jews being bound fast in their sins, all the world becomes subject to God. For no one doubts that the pagans are overwhelmed with offences and ungodliness, and so the whole world lies prostrate before God, that it may come to pardon: for the whole world was Jews and Gentiles, from whom the faithful have been set apart. When, then, he proves the Jews — who received the law from God, and to whom the promise was made — to be liable for their sins, there is no doubt that all the Greeks are guilty of death. That is why he says, *That every mouth may be stopped, and all the world may become subject to God*: since all, being proved guilty, need the mercy of God, Jews and Greeks alike.

*(Verse 20.) Therefore by the deeds of the law there shall no flesh be justified in his sight.* He does not say that men are not justified before God because they have not kept the law of righteousness in its precepts, but because they would not believe the mystery of God's sacrament which is in Christ. For it is through this that God decreed to justify man, not through the law; because the law justifies for a time, not before God. So those who keep the law in time are just, but not before God: because the faith through which all are justified before God is not in them. For faith is greater than the law: because the law concerns us, but faith concerns God. Therefore the law has a righteousness of time, faith an eternal one. In saying "all flesh", he meant every man; as the prophet Isaiah too says, *And all flesh shall see the salvation of God* (Isaiah 40:5): that is, every man shall see God's Christ, in whom the salvation of all is contained. And they are called flesh because they are liable to sin. For as righteousness makes men spiritual, so offences make them carnal; so that man takes his name from his doing. *For by the law is the knowledge of sin*: but by faith its abolishing; and therefore faith is to be followed. What is this law, through which he says sin was known — and known how? For we see that the men of old were not ignorant of sin: since Joseph was sent to prison, though by a false accusation (Genesis 39:20); and Pharaoh's butler and baker for a fault (Genesis 40:3). How, then, were sins hidden? The law is threefold: the first part concerns the sacrament of the Godhead of God; the second is that which agrees with the natural law, which forbids sin; and the third is of deeds, that is, of the sabbath, the new moon, circumcision, and the rest. This, then, is the natural law, which, partly renewed through Moses, and partly having its authority strengthened in restraining vices, made sin known: not that it was hidden, as I said, but it showed that the sins committed would not go unpunished before God; lest anyone, escaping for a time, should be thought to have made sport of the law. This is what the law showed.

*(Verse 21.) But now the righteousness of God without the law is manifested, being witnessed by the law and the prophets.* It is plain that the righteousness of God has appeared without the Law — but without the law of the sabbath and circumcision and the new moon and vengeance; not, however, without the sacrament of the Godhead of God, since the righteousness of God is from the sacrament of God. For when the righteousness of God pardoned those whom the law held guilty, it certainly did this without the law: for it forgave the sin of those on whom the law would have taken vengeance. And lest this should perhaps be thought to have been done against the law, he added that the righteousness of God has witness from the Law and the prophets: that is, that the Law itself, in another part, had long ago said that this would come — that one would come who would save men (Isaiah 19:20), which had not been granted to the law, namely to forgive sins. And it is called the righteousness of God, though it seems to be mercy, because it has its origin in a promise; and when God's promise is fulfilled, it is called the righteousness of God. For it is righteousness, because what was promised has been rendered. And when He receives those who flee to Him for refuge, it is called righteousness: because not to receive one who flees for refuge is unrighteousness.

*(Verse 22.) Even the righteousness of God which is by faith of Jesus Christ.* What does *by faith of Jesus Christ* mean, but that this very righteousness of God is the manifestation of Christ? For through faith in the preaching of Jesus Christ the gift long promised by God is recognised, or taken up. *Unto all and upon all them that believe*: that is, upon all the Jews and Greeks who believe — the righteousness of the true God.

*(Verse 23.) For there is no difference: for all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God.* Because he had said that the righteousness of God was upon all Jews and Greeks, he added to prove it, *For all*, he says, *have sinned.* This is to be taken generally, so that Jews as well as Greeks are understood; which is why he says, *There is no difference*. For here he includes all, the priests too, to show that the law had been of no profit without faith. For the law was given so that there should be faith in the law, which hoped for the salvation to come. Therefore the death of Christ profited all: for here in this world He taught what is to be believed and observed, and He rescued all from hell.

*(Verse 24.) Being justified freely by his grace.* They are justified freely, because, doing nothing and making no return, they are justified by faith alone, by the gift of God. *Through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus.* He testifies that the grace of God is in Christ: because by the will of God we have been redeemed by Christ, so that, set free, we might be justified; as he says to the Galatians as well, *Christ hath redeemed us* (Galatians 3:13), offering Himself for us. For He gave Himself up to the raging devil, but the devil did not foresee it. Thinking that he could hold Christ, he took Him as it were in hand; but because he could not bear His power, he lost, together with Him, all whom he held.

*(Verse 25.) Whom God hath set forth to be a propitiation through faith.* He says this because in Christ God set forth — that is, determined — that He would be propitious to the human race, if they believe. *In his blood*: in His blood, because we were set free by His death; so that He might both make Him manifest as dead, and condemn death by His passion.

*(Verse 26.) To declare his righteousness*: that is, to make plain His promise, by which He would set us free from sins, as He had promised before; and in fulfilling it, He showed Himself just. *For the remission of sins that are past, through the forbearance of God.* God, knowing the purpose of His goodness, by which He had determined to come to the help of sinners — both those who are among the living and those who were held in the underworld — waited a very long time for both, making void the sentence by which it seems just that all should be condemned; so as to show us that He had long ago decreed to set the human race free through Christ, as He promised through the prophet Jeremiah, saying, *I will be merciful to their iniquities, and their sins will I remember no more* (Jeremiah 31:34). And lest He should seem to have promised this gift to the Jews alone, He says through Isaiah, *Mine house shall be called an house of prayer for all people* (Isaiah 56:7). For though the promise was made to Judaism, yet God, foreknowing that the ungodly Jews would reject His gift, promised that He would admit the Gentiles to receive His grace; so that the ungodly Jews, provoked to anger by rivalry with them, should be tormented. *At this time*: that is, in our time, in which God has given what He had long ago promised would be given at the time at which He gave it. *That he might be just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus.* He says rightly that, that He might be just, He gave what He promised; and what He promised was this, to justify those who believe in Christ. For He says in Habakkuk, *The just shall live by faith* (Habakkuk 2:4): so that, while a man has faith in God and in Christ, he is just.

*(Verse 27.) Where is boasting then? It is excluded. By what law? of works? Nay: but by the law of faith.* Having given his reason, he speaks to those who live under the law, that they glory without cause, flattering themselves on the law and on being of the race of Abraham, when they see that a man is not justified before God except by faith.

*(Verse 28.) Therefore we conclude that a man is justified by faith without the deeds of the law.* He says that it is to be held for certain that the Gentile is justified by believing, doing none of the works of the law: that is, without circumcision, or new moons, or reverence for the sabbath.

*(Verse 29.) Is he the God of the Jews only? is he not also of the Gentiles? Yes, of the Gentiles also.* Without doubt the one God is the God of all. For the Jews alone cannot claim for themselves that their God is not the God of the Gentiles as well, since they read that the origin of all is from one Adam, and that no stranger who wished to come to the law was forbidden. Indeed, a number of Egyptians went out with them into the wilderness, and they were commanded to receive them (Exodus 12:38 ff.), provided only that they were circumcised, and ate the unleavened bread and the passover together with them. And besides, Cornelius, a Gentile who did not keep the Jewish rites, received the gift of God; and that he was justified was confirmed by divine witness (Acts 10:31).

*(Verse 30.) Seeing it is one God, which shall justify the circumcision by faith, and uncircumcision through faith.* By the circumcision he means the Jews, in that they were justified by faith in the promise, believing that this was the Christ whom God had promised in the law; by the uncircumcision he means the Gentiles, justified before God through faith in Christ. For He justified Gentiles and Jews alike in no other way than as believers: for since there is one God of all, He justified all by one rule. What, then, does circumcision of the flesh profit, or what harm does uncircumcision do, when nothing but faith makes worth and merit?

*(Verse 31.) Do we then make void the law through faith? God forbid: yea, we establish the law.* Certainly the law is not emptied by faith, but fulfilled: for its standing is confirmed when faith testifies that what the law said would come has come. This is said on account of the Jews, who think that faith in Christ is hostile to the law, not knowing the meaning of the law. He does not make the law void, then, when he teaches that it must now cease: for he declares that it was rightly given in its own time, but that it ought no longer to be in force. For it was said in the law itself that the law must cease at the time when the promise was fulfilled. For the angel Gabriel showed the prophet Daniel, when he longed to know this (Daniel 9:25), saying among other things that when Christ came the chrism, that is, the royal anointing, by which power is understood, would cease. *And judgment*, he says, *shall be no more* — which is the law — *and my sacrifice shall be taken away.* He says "my", so that it might be known that the old offering would cease. And the Saviour, opening this sense, says, *The law and the prophets were until John* (Luke 16:16). And because, when the law of Moses ceased, God would give better commandments, the prophet Jeremiah sang, saying, *Behold, the days come, saith the Lord, when I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel, and with the house of Judah: not according to the covenant that I made with their fathers* (Jeremiah 31:31–32): with those, that is, who received Christ when He came according to the promise. So the Saviour says, *I am not come to destroy the law, or the prophets, but to fulfil* (Matthew 5:17). And because the house of God was to be not only in Jerusalem alone, but in other places as well, the prophet Zechariah says, *I will set Jerusalem among all the nations* (Zechariah 12:2): which is the Church.
