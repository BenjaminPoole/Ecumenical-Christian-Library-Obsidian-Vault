# Commentary on Romans, Chapter 4

*While almost all of the works in this vault use Victorian translations, due to the English translations of this commentary being in copyright, it was machine-translated (by Claude, 2026) from the public-domain Latin. It has not been checked by a human translator.*

*Commentary on Paul's Epistle to the Romans, long printed among the works of Ambrose. Its author, whom Erasmus first called "Ambrosiaster", wrote at Rome while Damasus was bishop there (366–384). Chapter 4 of the Epistle; the verse numbers are the Benedictine editors'.*

*(Verse 1.) What shall we say then that Abraham our father, as pertaining to the flesh, hath found?* After showing that no one can be justified before God through the works of the law, he added that not even Abraham could merit anything according to the flesh. By "flesh" he meant circumcision: for Abraham sought nothing by circumcision. For he was justified before he was circumcised.

*(Verse 2.) For if Abraham were justified by the works of the Law, he hath whereof to glory; but not before God.* This is said by way of supposition. For that Abraham was justified by faith no one doubts; and so he has glory before God. But because those who keep the law — whether the law of Moses or the natural law — are justified for the present, so as not to be guilty in the present judgment, he says: "If Abraham was justified by the works of the law, he has no glory before God." For *he that doeth the law*, it says, *shall live in it*: that is, he shall not die as one guilty; yet he will not have merit with God from this, but from faith.

*(Verse 3.) For what saith the Scripture? Abraham believed God, and it was counted unto him for righteousness* (Genesis 15:6). He has made it plain that Abraham has glory before God not because he was circumcised, nor because he kept himself from wickedness, but because he believed God: and from this he was justified, and will obtain the reward of his praise in the world to come.

*(Verse 4.) Now to him that worketh is the reward not reckoned of grace, but of debt.* It is settled that for him who is subject to the law of deeds, that is, of Moses, or to the natural law, merit is not reckoned towards a reward, so that he should have glory before God. For he is a debtor to do the law, because a necessity has been laid on him by the law: so that, willing or unwilling, he must do the law, lest he be condemned; as he says in another place, *They that resist shall receive to themselves damnation* (Romans 13:2), because they are guilty for the present. But to believe or not to believe is a matter of the will. For no one can be compelled to what is not plain to see; he is invited: for faith is not wrung out of a man; he is persuaded to it. And therefore he who assents is to be rewarded, as Abraham was, who believed what he did not see.

*(Verse 5.) But to him that worketh not* — that is, to him who is liable for his sins, who does not do what the law commands — *but believeth on him that justifieth the ungodly, his faith is counted for righteousness.* He says this because, to the ungodly man — that is, the Gentile — who believes in Christ without the works of the law, his faith is counted for righteousness, as Abraham's was. How, then, do the Jews think they are justified through the works of the law by the justification of Abraham, when they see that Abraham was justified not through the works of the law, but by faith alone? There is no need of the law, then, when the ungodly is justified before God by faith alone. *According to the purpose of the grace of God.* He says that God has so decreed that, the law ceasing, the grace of God should require faith alone for salvation.

*(Verse 6.) Even as David also describeth.* He fortifies this very point with the example of the prophet. *The blessedness of the man, unto whom God imputeth righteousness without works.* He calls those blessed concerning whom God has ordained this: that, without toil and without any observance, they should be justified before God by faith alone. So he proclaims the blessedness of the time in which Christ was born, as the Lord Himself says, *Many prophets and righteous men have desired to see those things which ye see, and have not seen them; and to hear those things which ye hear, and have not heard them* (Matthew 13:17).

*(Verses 7–8.) Blessed are they whose iniquities are forgiven, and whose sins are covered. Blessed is the man to whom the Lord will not impute sin* (Psalm 32:1–2). Plainly they are blessed whose iniquities are forgiven, and whose sins are covered, without toil or any work, no works of penance being asked of them, but only that they believe. Blessed too is the man to whom the Lord has not imputed sin. To forgive, to cover, and not to impute are one account and one meaning: for all are obtained and given in one way. Some, however, see a threefold account in these sayings, because the prophet used different words, and because he goes down from the plural number to the singular. For when he wishes to set out the words of God for the abundance of grace, he has spoken at greater length, since there are different names for sin, through the terms for offences; yet the words are of one form and meaning, since when He covers, He forgives, and when He forgives, He does not impute. For he speaks at greater length in praise of the grace of God, to magnify the grace. He made three steps because of the variety of offences: of which the first step is iniquity or ungodliness, when the Creator is not acknowledged; the second lies in the works of grave sins; and the third in those of light ones; yet all these are blotted out in baptism. By these three steps he signified the whole body of sin. How, though, can it apply to the person of penitents, when he says, *Blessed are they whose sins are covered*, since it is well known that penitents gain the forgiveness of sins with toil and groaning? Or how does it fit martyrdom when he says, *Blessed is the man to whom the Lord hath not imputed sin*, since we know that the glory of martyrdom is gained by sufferings and afflictions? But the prophet, foreseeing the happy time of the Saviour's coming, calls those blessed whose sins are forgiven, and covered, and not imputed, through the washing, without toil or any work. Yet the Apostle, because of the fulness of the times, and because there is more grace in the apostles than there was in the prophets, declares greater things, which we obtain from the gift of baptism: for he declares that we not only receive forgiveness of sins, but are justified and made sons of God; so that this blessedness may have both perfect security and glory.

*(Verse 9.) Cometh this blessedness then upon the circumcision only, or upon the uncircumcision also?* That is: has this blessedness been granted only to the sons of Abraham, or to those who are uncircumcised as well? For if at that time the Gentiles were not forbidden to come to the law and the promise of Abraham, how could it come about that in the time of Christ those coming to grace should be forbidden, when God has urged them on fully by grace? *For we say* — that is, if we speak according to the sense of the law — *that faith was reckoned to Abraham for righteousness.* He begins from the source, to shut out every shift of cavilling: for one who is shut out at the head has nowhere to begin raising a question.

*(Verses 10–11.) How was it then reckoned? when he was in circumcision, or in uncircumcision? Not in circumcision, but in uncircumcision. And he received the sign of circumcision, a seal of the righteousness of the faith which he had yet being uncircumcised: that he might be the father of all them that believe, though they be not circumcised; that righteousness might be imputed unto them also* (Genesis 17:10–11). Abraham, while he was in uncircumcision, believed God. What did he believe? That he would have seed, that is, a son, in whom all nations should be justified by faith in uncircumcision, as Abraham too was justified. For he received circumcision as a sign of the righteousness of faith: for, believing that he would have a son, he received a sign of the thing he believed, so that it might be seen that he had been justified by believing. Circumcision, then, has no dignity of its own; it is only a sign. The sons of Abraham received this sign so that they might be known to be his sons who had received it in believing God; that they might be imitators of their father's faith, and believe in Jesus, who was promised to Abraham, and in whose figure Isaac was born. For it is not in Isaac that all nations are blessed, but in Christ: *For there is none other name under heaven given, whereby we must be saved* (Acts 4:12), says Peter the apostle.

*(Verse 12.) And the father of circumcision to them who are not of the circumcision only, but who also walk in the steps of that faith of our father Abraham, which he had being yet uncircumcised.* He says this because Abraham, by believing, became the first father of circumcision — but of the heart — not only of those who are of his stock, but also of those from the Gentiles who believe in the same way. For according to the flesh he is the father of the Jews, but according to faith, of all who believe.

*(Verse 13.) For the promise, that he should be the heir of the world, was not to Abraham, or to his seed, through the law, but through the righteousness of faith.* It is plain that neither had the Law yet been given, nor was there yet circumcision, when the promise was made to Abraham as a believer, and to his seed, which is Christ, who would wash away the sins of men. Hence John the Baptist says, *Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world* (John 1:29). So it was not by the merit of a law kept, but of faith, that he was made heir of the world. And the heir of the world is heir of the land, which he obtained in his sons; but Christ is heir of the nations, as David sings, *I shall give thee the heathen for thine inheritance, and the uttermost parts of the earth for thy possession* (Psalm 2:8); for we ourselves shall die and live with Him.

*(Verse 14.) For if they which are of the law be heirs* — that is, if those who are of the law, or under the law, are heirs by the law — *faith is made void, and the promise made of none effect.* It is plain that, if the inheritance is by the law, the promise which was made to Abraham by faith comes to nothing. But the promise was not made through the Law; the inheritance is through the righteousness of faith. For the Apostle shows what an evil it is to hope for the inheritance from the law.

*(Verse 15.) Because the law worketh wrath.* To show thoroughly that a man cannot be justified before God through the law, and that the promise could not have been made through the law, he says, *The law worketh wrath*: for it was given for this, to make offenders guilty. But faith is the gift of God's mercy, so that those made guilty through the law may obtain pardon; and therefore faith works joy. He is not speaking against the law, but setting faith before it: because those who could not be saved through the law are saved by the grace of God through faith. The law, then, is not wrath, but works wrath, that is, punishment, for the sinner, since it does not pardon but avenges. For punishment is begotten of wrath, and wrath is born of sin; and so he wishes the law to be left, that men may take refuge in faith, which pardons offences so as to save. *For where no law is, there is no transgression.* He says this because, when the guilty have been taken out of the power of the law and pardon given, there is no transgression: for those who had been sinners through transgression of the law have been justified. For the law of deeds ceases: that is, the observance of sabbaths and new moons and circumcision, the distinction of foods, and the observance concerning a dead animal, or the blood of a weasel.

*(Verse 16.) Therefore it is of faith, that it might be by grace; to the end the promise might be sure to all the seed.* The promise cannot be sure to all the seed — that is, to every man of every nation — unless it is of faith: because the origin of the promise is of faith, not of the law; because those who are under the law are guilty, and the promise cannot be given to the guilty. And so they must first be purified by faith, that they may become worthy to be called sons of God, and the promise may be sure. For if they call themselves sons of God while they are guilty — that is, under the law — it is not sure: because the sons of God are free from sin. If, then, those who are under the law must be taken out from under the law in order to deserve to receive the promise, how much more those who are not under the law! It is not to one's advantage, then, to put oneself under the law, when one can find the cure of one's wound by a short way. *Not to that only which is of the law, but to that also which is of the faith of Abraham.* This is what he said above, *to all the seed*: of the Jews, who came of course from the law, as well as of the Gentiles, who follow the faith of Abraham — the faith which he had as a Gentile, so as to be just. For Abraham believed without the law, and so he is joined rather to the Gentiles; so that the promise may be sure to them, believing in Him in whom he himself believed.

*(Verse 17.) Who is the father of us all, (as it is written, I have made thee a father of many nations* (Genesis 17:4).) He has confirmed by an example from the Law that Abraham is the father of all, but of those who believe; and that the promise is sure in this way, if one withdraws from the law for the sake of faith. For the promise of the kingdom of heaven has been assigned to the just, not to sinners. And those who are under the law are under sin, because all have sinned; and no one can both be under the law and obtain grace, as he says to the Galatians too: *Christ is become of no effect unto you, whosoever of you are justified by the law; ye are fallen from grace* (Galatians 5:4). *Before him whom he believed, even God.* To teach that there is one God of all, he tells the Gentiles that Abraham believed this very God, and was justified before Him in whom the Gentiles too believe, that they may be justified; so that there is no difference between Jew and Greek in faith: because, circumcision and uncircumcision being taken away, they are made one in Christ; for Abraham too believed in uncircumcision, and was justified. *Who quickeneth the dead, and calleth those things which be not as though they were.* By saying this he invites the Gentiles to the faith of Abraham: because when he was in uncircumcision, believing God — faith in whom is now preached in Christ — he was quickened together with his wife. For when they were worn out with age, they came to life again in their old age; so that he did not doubt that he would have a son by Sarah, whom he knew to be barren, and in whom the way of women had already ceased. So by this they may care neither for uncircumcision nor for circumcision, and be ready for faith; secure because they believe Him who quickens the dead, and that there is no other who has this power; and when He wills things to be that are not, at once they come to be by His will. So Abraham, when he was not yet a father, was called the father of many nations, and believed, secure in the power of God. And that the God whom Abraham believed might be believed to be the same who is the Father of Christ, a sign was given in Zacharias and Elisabeth (Luke 1:13), when the promise was being fulfilled in the world and Christ was at hand, such as had been given in Abraham and Sarah when the promise was sealed; so that in the same way bodies dead with old age should beget Saint John, as Isaac too had been born.

*(Verse 18.) Who against hope believed in hope* (Genesis 15:5). It is plain that, while Abraham had no hope of begetting, he believed God, having faith against hope that he would beget, knowing that God can do all things. *That he might become the father of many nations, according to that which was spoken, So shall thy seed be* (Genesis 15:5). This is in Genesis: for, showing him the stars of heaven, He said, *So shall thy seed be*; and believing it, he was justified. For he believed what seems impossible to the world, since it does not fall within the nature of things: that the old should be able to beget, and that their seed should be multiplied in such numbers that it could not be counted. And so faith is precious, because it believes that what is contrary to what one knows or sees will come to pass. It consoles itself with this hope, that it is God who promises, of whom it is right to think more than human weakness can grasp.

*(Verses 19–22.) And being not weak in faith, he considered not his own body now dead, when he was about an hundred years old, neither yet the deadness of Sara's womb: he staggered not at the promise of God through unbelief; but was strong in faith, giving glory to God; and being fully persuaded that, what he had promised, he was able also to perform. And therefore it was imputed to him for righteousness.* He proclaims this in Abraham as worthy of praise: that, knowing he could not, he strengthened his weakness by faith, so as to believe he could, through God, what he knew could not be done by the decrees of the world. He is of great merit before God, then, who believed God against his own knowledge, not doubting that He could do — as being God — what he knows cannot be done by the reckoning of the world. For he holds it certain that God is above the reckoning of the world: for no one is to be said to be inside what he has created. So a man of this kind is to be rewarded by God, because he ascribes to his Author more than he himself understands. This would indeed be of no worth if all thought alike; but the faith of believers is commended before God by the unbelief of many. He exhorts the Gentiles, then, to this firmness of faith, that they may receive God's promise and grace without any doubting, secure by the example of Abraham: because the praise of the believer grows from this, if what is incredible and seems foolish to the world is believed; because the more impossible what is believed is thought to be, the more honourable the believer will be. And yet it would truly be foolish to believe this, if it were said to be done without God. And so the faith of Abraham is the more praiseworthy than the faith of others, because it was drawn by no signs and wonders. And since the world is governed by a fixed law and power, and ruled in a way laid down by God; and men, eager for reasoning out of love of the flesh, neglected God its Maker: God, to take away their error and show Himself Lord of all, willed it to be proclaimed that He could do and had done what is impossible to the world; so that those who believed this, set apart from those who, puffed up by the reasoning of the world, neglected God and are condemned, might be saved, given over into the dominion of God.

*(Verses 23–25.) Now it was not written for his sake alone, that it was imputed to him* (Genesis 15:6); *but for us also, to whom it shall be imputed, if we believe on him that raised up Jesus Christ our Lord from the dead; who was delivered for our offences, and was raised again for our justification.* He says that in Abraham a pattern was given to Jews and Gentiles, so that by his example we should believe in God, and in Christ, and in the Holy Spirit, and righteousness be reckoned to us. Though what is believed now is different, yet faith has one gift. We obtain, then, what we believe: for, believing that Christ is the Son of God, we are adopted by God as sons; for He could give believers nothing beyond being called sons of God, the faithless being disowned. For we are called sons of God, while they are not worthy even to be called servants. For of His infinite magnificence God has given to those who love Him what is worthy of His majesty, not what men deserve: for in a gift the greatness of the giver is regarded more than that of the receiver, and He receives more praise from this, when He bestows great gifts on little ones through Christ, who suffered Himself to be killed for our sake, that, pardon being given, He might rescue us from the second death, that is, from the punishment of hell; and He rose again, so that, by the joy of His triumph, death being overcome, He might give us the grace of justification, that we might be worthy to be called sons of God. For those who were baptized before the Passion received only the forgiveness of sins — sins through zeal for which Satan killed the Saviour. But after the resurrection, those who were baptized before as well as those baptized afterwards were all justified through the pattern of faith in the Trinity which was given, and by receiving the Holy Spirit, who is the sign that believers are sons of God. And that it might serve to crown our justification, in rising He gave authority to His commandments; so that, emulating them, we might enrich ourselves with the merits by which, having gained glory, we may appear illustrious in the kingdom of God, in this confidence: that, having been justified, we cannot be held by death. For death, conquered by the Saviour's Passion — death, which before reigned because of sin — dares not hold back those whom He has justified.
