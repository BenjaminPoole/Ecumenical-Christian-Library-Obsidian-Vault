# Commentary on Romans, Chapter 8

*While almost all of the works in this vault use Victorian translations, due to the English translations of this commentary being in copyright, it was machine-translated (by Claude, 2026) from the public-domain Latin. It has not been checked by a human translator.*

*Commentary on Paul's Epistle to the Romans, long printed among the works of Ambrose. Its author, whom Erasmus first called "Ambrosiaster", wrote at Rome while Damasus was bishop there (366–384). Chapter 8 of the Epistle; the verse numbers are the Benedictine editors'.*

*(Verses 1–2.) There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus, who walk not after the flesh.* It is true that there is no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus, carefully serving the law of God. *For the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus hath made me free from the law of sin and death.* He insists on security for man through the grace of God, that he need not be anxious about the suggestions of the devil, so long as he scorns them: for they will do man no harm with regard to the second death, because the law of faith, that is, of the spirit, has set man free from the second death, sin being condemned. Nor does it now harm a man that sin is in his flesh, provided he fights against it, mindful of God's help; rather he is to be crowned who presses down the counsels of the sin that remains in the flesh: for it is great skill to avoid the snares of an enemy in one's own house. *The law of the Spirit of life*, then, is the law of faith. For the law of Moses too is spiritual, because it forbids sinning; yet it is not "of life", because it cannot forgive the sins of those guilty of death, so as to make alive those who are to die. But this law, because it both does not wish men to sin and calls them back from death, is called the law of the Spirit of life: it does not stand by the letter but by the spirit, because it is believed with the heart, and what is believed is spirit. So this Law, *in Christ Jesus* — that is, through faith in Christ — sets the believer free *from the law of sin and death.* The law of sin is the one he says dwells in the members, which tries to urge on us what is contrary; and the law of death is the law of Moses, because it puts sinners to death. For in another epistle too he says among other things, of the authority of the law, *If the ministration of death, written and engraven in stones, was glorious* (2 Corinthians 3:7). It is no wonder, then, if the law is called spiritual, and the law of death, since the Gospel is so too. For he says in another place among other things, *To the one we are the savour of death unto death; and to the other the savour of life unto life* (2 Corinthians 2:16). For to those whom the report of mighty works, or the sight or hearing of things done, drew to the faith, the Gospel was the savour of life; but to those whose minds the power that had been shown inflamed to contradiction, the preaching of the faith was the savour of death. Though faith is one, then, it brings about different results in men; just as the sun, though it is one, melts wax and hardens mud. With what mind, then, a man has caught the scent of faith, so will it profit him. But perhaps it will be said: "If faith does the same as the Law, why is not faith too called in part a law of death? For it puts unbelievers to death." But it is not so. For faith was given to justify those who take refuge in it: to forgive those whom the Law holds guilty, so that those who live under faith may be free from sin; whereas those who live under the law are liable. Those, then, who do not obey faith are not killed by faith, but by the Law: because, not coming to faith, they are held guilty by the Law; and so all who scent the words of the faith with an evil mind remain in death. Let us see now what difference there is when one speaks of "a spiritual law" and "the law of the Spirit". The difference is this: it is called a spiritual law because it gives commandments by which men may not sin, since he who does not sin is called spiritual, as one who imitates the things above, that is, the heavenly. But it is called the law of the Spirit because God, whose faith it is, is spirit. In the one, then, there are words; in the other, the thing itself: in the one, the things of God; in the other, God Himself.

*(Verse 3.) For what the Law could not do, in that it was weak through the flesh, God sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh, and for sin, condemned sin in the flesh.* He says this to make the baptized secure, because they have been set free from sin. *For what the Law could not do*, he says. To whom was it impossible? To us, of course, it was impossible to fulfil the commandment of the Law, because we were subject to sin. For this reason God sent His Son in the likeness of sinful flesh. This is the likeness of the flesh: that, though it is the same flesh as ours, yet it was not made in the womb and born as our flesh is. For it was sanctified in the womb, and born without sin, and He Himself did not sin in it. For this reason a virgin's womb was chosen for the Lord's birth: that the Lord's flesh should differ from our flesh in holiness. For it is like ours in its cause, not in the quality of sin in the substance. He called it "like", then, because, being of the same substance of flesh, it did not have the same birth: for the Lord's body was not subject to sin. For the Lord's flesh was cleansed by the Holy Spirit, so that He should be born in such a body as Adam's was before sin — only the sentence given against Adam remaining. God, then, having sent Christ, *for sin condemned sin*: that is, He condemned sin by its own sin. For when Christ is crucified by sin, which is Satan, sin sinned in the flesh of the Saviour's body; and by this God condemned sin in the flesh — there, that is, where it sinned; as he says in another epistle too, *triumphing over them in it* (Colossians 2:15), that is, in Christ. For it is usual to say of any condemned man, "On what charge was he condemned?" And the answer is, say, "For murder." So sin too was condemned in the flesh, that is, for the sin which it committed in the flesh. Made guilty, then, by this sin, Satan lost his dominion over the souls he held, so that he no longer dares to hold in the second death those who are sealed with the sign of the cross, on which he was conquered. That is why the Apostle treats these things, for our security.

*(Verse 4.) That the righteousness of the Law might be fulfilled in us, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit.* He says that sin has been condemned so that the righteousness of the law given by Moses might be fulfilled in us: for, taken out of the condition of the Law, we have become its friends, since the justified are friends of the Law. And how is justification fulfilled in us, but when the forgiveness of all sins is given; so that, sins being taken away, one appears justified, serving the law of God with the mind? This is to walk not after the flesh, but after the spirit: that the devotion of the mind, which is spirit, should not consent to the desire of sin, since the sin that is within sows its lusts through the flesh. For if sin has been condemned by the Saviour, how is it within? It has been condemned by the Saviour, and condemned in three ways. In the first place He condemned sin when He did not sin, refusing to agree with sin. Next, sin is said to have been condemned on the cross, because it sinned; and so its authority, as of sin, was taken from it, by which it held men in the underworld for Adam's offence; so that for the future it does not dare to hold those on whom is the sign of the cross. Thirdly, He condemned sin when He made offences void, granting the forgiveness of sins. For when the sinner deserved to be condemned for the sin he had committed, by pardoning him He condemned the sin in him. So if we too, after the Saviour's example, do not sin, we condemn sin.

*(Verse 5.) For they that are after the flesh do mind the things of the flesh.* He says this because he who obeys the suggestion that is made through the flesh minds the things of the flesh. For what he follows seems sweet to him, as he desires, against what is right, the practice and opinion of the flesh's error: for by "flesh" the error of the world is signified, in whatever part. *But they that are after the Spirit, the things of the Spirit.* It is plain that he who keeps himself from the desires of the flesh lives according to the law of the mind: for it seems good to him to mind what agrees with the Law.

*(Verse 6.) For to be carnally minded is death.* The prudence of the flesh is sin, which begets death. It is called prudence, though it is a foolish thing, because to worldly men the errors conceived from visible things, whether in thought or in act, against the law of God, seem to be prudence: above all because all their industry and cunning goes into sinning. For they think themselves wise if they attend to this the more diligently, when nothing is more foolish than to sin. There is also another prudence of the flesh, which, puffed up by worldly reasonings, denies that anything is done which lacks the reason of the world: hence it laughs at the Virgin's childbearing and the resurrection of the flesh. *But to be spiritually minded is life and peace.* This is truly wisdom, which gains life and peace: for he who follows spiritual things, despising the allurements of the present life, will have eternal life with peace, that is, without disquiet, where there is no disturbance and no punishment.

*(Verse 7.) Because the carnal mind is enmity against God: for it is not subject to the law of God, neither indeed can be.* He did not call the flesh an enemy, but the wisdom of the flesh: that is, not the substance, but either evil deeds, or a thought or assertion that is born of error. The wisdom of the flesh, then, is first of all the reasoning about the stars devised by men, and then the delight in visible things. These are hostile to God, because they make the Lord of the elements and Maker of the world equal to the things He made, asserting that nothing can be done except what the reason of the world contains. That is why they deny that God brought it about that a Virgin bore a child, or that the bodies of the dead rise again: "for it is foolish", they say, "that God should have done more than man understands." O prudent ones of the world, who think that God ought not to act otherwise than the creation founded by Him acts — so that He Himself is thought to be like His creatures! So blinded are they that they do not see what an insult they offer to God. For the work which He deigned to do for the proclaiming of His praise, they find fault with and call incredible and foolish. And so this prudence of the flesh cannot be subject to God's law, since they sharpen their zeal for this very purpose, to fight against God's deeds.

*(Verses 8–9.) So then they that are in the flesh cannot please God.* The wise of the world are in the flesh, because they study the wisdom of the world, by which they fight against God's law. For whatever is against the law of God is carnal, because it is of the world; for the whole world is flesh: everything visible is counted to the flesh, for these things are akin to the flesh — or rather, are the elements of the flesh. And so he who obeys the things of the world is in the flesh. *But ye are not in the flesh, but in the Spirit.* Though set in the flesh, they are said not to be in the flesh if, agreeing with the apostle John, they do not love the things of the world: for a man's way of thinking, as it were, fashions his nature, so that he is called by what he thinks. *If so be that the Spirit of God dwell in you.* He says this doubtfully because, having been brought into the Law, they were not yet perfect in faith; but he saw in them the hope of perfection. So he speaks to them sometimes as perfect, sometimes as those who will be perfect: that is, sometimes he praises, sometimes he admonishes; so that, if they live according to what was mentioned above, by the law of nature, they may be said to be in the spirit: because the Spirit of God cannot dwell in one who follows carnal things. *Now if any man have not the Spirit of Christ, he is none of his.* Him whom above he called the Spirit of God, he now calls the Spirit of Christ, because all things of the Father are the Son's. He says this, then: that he who is subject to the errors mentioned above does not belong to Christ: for he does not have His Spirit, whom he had received in order to be a son of God. For the Holy Spirit forsakes a man for two reasons: when he has either the mind of the flesh or its deeds. So by these admonitions he exhorts them to a good way of life: for though men are raised up by the goodness of God, yet those called sons of God are burdened unless they so live as not to seem unworthy of the name granted them.

*(Verse 10.) And if Christ be in you, the body is dead because of sin; but the Spirit is life because of righteousness.* He asserts that the body of those whom the Holy Spirit forsakes is dead because of sin, and that the suffering of their death does not reach this one, that is, the Spirit. For the Spirit of God does not know how to sin: for He is given for justification, to justify by His help. Since He does not know how to sin, then, He is life: for He cannot die, since death is through sin. And so the sinner will harm himself, not the Spirit whom he received. For the Spirit, who wishes to justify, will not be to blame. For this is the sign of justification in a man: that through Him who dwells in him he appears, being justified, to be a son of God. For the Holy Spirit cannot dwell in a deceitful man, nor in a body subject to sins, as Solomon says (Wisdom 1:4–5); and if anyone lives carnally again, forsaken by the Holy Spirit, he will die in his unrighteousness. In saying "the body", he means the whole man, dead because of sin; as also by the prophet the whole man is meant under the word "soul", the whole from the part; for he says, *The soul that sinneth, it shall die* (Ezekiel 18:4). Without the body? The one put "body" for the whole man, the other "soul". And another prophet, saying "flesh", meant the whole man; for he says, *All flesh shall see the salvation of God* (Isaiah 40:5). And since the Holy Spirit is given to the baptized, He is said to be the spirit of the baptized man himself. So he called the rest of the man "body" by comparison with Him, because the soul, when it sins, is called flesh: for it is called by what it follows, as I have often said already. In the same way the Lord too, when in His Passion He wished it to be understood that His Godhead did not fear at all, but that the sadness was the man's, says, *The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak* (Matthew 26:41): to signify God in the spirit and man in the flesh.

*(Verse 11.) But if the Spirit of him that raised up Jesus from the dead dwell in you, he that raised up Christ from the dead shall also quicken your mortal bodies by his Spirit that dwelleth in you.* He treats the same sense in the same way: for he said "bodies", meaning men. And since above he said that the body dies the second death because of sin, here again he has promised that, because of a good life, mortal bodies will be made alive — that is, the whole man.

*(Verse 12.) Therefore, brethren, we are debtors, not to the flesh, to live after the flesh.* It is right and plain that we ought not to comply with what was devised by Adam, who acted carnally and, sinning first, left death to us by right of inheritance; but that we ought to serve the law of Christ, who by a spiritual means redeemed us from the death spoken of above. For we are debtors to Him who, when we were befouled with carnal vices, washed us by the washing of the Spirit, justified us, and made us sons of God. For formerly, set in the flesh, we lived after Adam's example, subject to sins; but now, set free, we ought to render our service to the Redeemer. And this service certainly does not profit Him, who needs nothing, but gains eternal life for us: for He so loves us as to count to Himself what profits us.

*(Verse 13.) For if ye live after the flesh, ye shall die.* Nothing is truer: for if we live after Adam, we shall die. For Adam, transgressing, sold himself to sin, being given over to the flesh. For all sin is flesh: because vices and offences that arise from without, from the senses — that is, from hearing, sight, touch, smell or taste — are counted to the flesh. For every thought that looks outward runs into offence: for the first man's sin too was born from without. To live after the flesh, then, is death: for every act of the flesh is outside the Law. *But if ye through the Spirit do mortify the deeds of the body, ye shall live.* He wishes the body to be governed by the law of the soul. So he shows that, if the deeds and counsels of the flesh, which are contrived at the instigation of the powers of this world, are held down with the Holy Spirit as guide, so that they do not get the power to act, life is bestowed. For they are said to be mortified if they cease: for they do not exist if they cease, since there is no sin if it is not done.

*(Verse 14.) For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, they are the sons of God.* He says that those are led by the Spirit of God in whose deeds the counsels of the princes and powers of this world are not seen. For those in whose deeds they are seen are not sons of God, but of the devil: because *whosoever is born of God doth not commit sin*, says the apostle John (1 John 3:9). For this is what proves sons of God and sons of the devil. Hence the Lord said to the Jews, because they did evil and were plotting murder, *Ye are of your father the devil* (John 8:44).

*(Verse 15.) For ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear.* He says this because, having received the Holy Spirit, we have been stripped of the fear of evil deeds; so that for the future we may do nothing of the kind, for which we should fear again. For before we were under fear, because, when the Law was given, all were made guilty. By the spirit of fear, then, he meant the Law, because it put men in fear because of sin. But the law of faith, which is meant by the spirit of adoption, is the Law of security: for it rescues us from fear when it forgives sins, and so has made us secure; and in this way it is called a spirit not of fear. *But ye have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father.* Set free from fear by the grace of God, we have received the spirit of adoption as sons; so that, considering what we were and what we have obtained by God's gift, we may order our life with great care, lest the name of God the Father suffer insult in us, and we, as ungrateful men, incur all the things we have escaped. For we have obtained such grace that we dare to say to God, "Abba", that is, "Father!" And so he warns that the confidence we have received must not be turned to rashness: for if we show a life unlike this cry by which we say "Abba, Father", we insult God by calling Him Father. For in His goodness He has granted us this, which is above our nature, so that what we are unworthy of by our substance we may deserve by our works.

*(Verse 16.) The Spirit itself beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God.* When we act well, and by this very thing the Holy Spirit remains in us, the Spirit of God bears witness to this cry and to our mind, with which we cry in prayer "Abba", that is, "Father", by remaining in us: witness that we do not say "Abba, Father" rashly. For let us show a life worthy of this cry; and this is the witness of sons, if the Father's sign is seen in them through the Spirit.

*(Verse 17.) And if children, then heirs; heirs of God, and joint-heirs with Christ.* Since God the Father can in no way be said to be dead, while Christ His Son is said to have died because of the incarnation, how is it that he who died is said to be heir of One who always lives, when heirs are of course heirs only of the dead? But this belongs to the manner of humanity, not of the Godhead. For with God, what is called an inheritance among us is the gift of the Father passed on to obedient sons; so that the living is heir of the living by his own merit, not by the necessity of one who has died. And so the Lord too in the Gospel showed, though in a parable, that a living man divided his substance among the living (Luke 15:12), by a divine rather than a human reckoning; for a parable is not put together on an absurd principle. To make us ready to obey God the Father, then, he exhorts us with this hope, saying that we shall be heirs of God and joint-heirs with Christ; so that, since the hope of reward is great, we might be the more inclined to the things of God, putting the care of worldly things second. And what it is to be joint-heir of the Son of God, we are taught by the apostle John; for he says among other things, *We know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him* (1 John 3:2). *If so be that we suffer with him, that we may be also glorified together.* He declares how we can become joint-heirs of Christ, saying, *If so be that we suffer with him, that we may be also glorified together.* Let us see, then, what it is to suffer with Him. To suffer with Him is to bear persecutions for the hope of what is to come, and to crucify the flesh with its vices and lusts: that is, to despise the pleasures and pomps of the world. For when all these have died in a man, he crucifies the world, believing in the life of the world to come, in which he hopes to be a joint-heir with Christ.

*(Verse 18.) For I reckon that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us.* This exhortation belongs to what went before: by it he shows that what the faithless can inflict here is small compared with the gift decreed in the world to come. Therefore he bids us be ready for every ruin, because great rewards have been promised for these things; so that in tribulation the mind may comfort itself and grow in hope. For we know some, indeed many, who for present gains — which will profit them only a little — undergo the harshest and hardest labours, and sometimes cannot reach what they aim at, because life itself is frail and uncertain. For what profits do sailors trust themselves to such storms and tempests, when they know that in these death is before their eyes rather than life? And soldiers, in the hope of a present reward, do not hesitate to rush on the swords, though victory is uncertain. How much more, then, must we suffer for Christ, whose benefits have even been bestowed on us beforehand: who promises great rewards for small, heavenly for earthly, everlasting for temporal, with glory! We are said to suffer for Christ, though what we suffer profits us, not Him: but He commands it because He seeks an occasion of rewarding us; for one who is good, or lavish, looks for a reason to give generously, either to the unworthy or to the modest. Indeed, so that these very ruins may be borne, He Himself supplies the strength.

*(Verse 19.) For the earnest expectation of the creature waiteth for the manifestation of the sons of God.* Having said that the sufferings of the present time are not worthy of the glory that is to come, he added that the creation waits for this: that the number of the sons of God destined for life may be made up; so that then at last the creation too may be set free from the bondage of corruption and cease from its defect.

*(Verse 20.) For the creature was made subject to vanity, not willingly.* The creation, set in the power of its Lord and Creator, was certainly not made subject to vanity of its own accord: for its being made subject will profit not itself but us. In what, then, is it subject to vanity, but that the things it brings forth are perishable? For it works so as to bear perishable fruits; and corruption itself is vanity: for all things that are born in the world are weak, perishable and corruptible, and so are vain. They are vain because they cannot keep their state: for all things, losing their form through flux, always return into themselves, merged into nature. Solomon too says of these things that all this is vanity (Ecclesiastes 1:2). Nor does David disagree with him, saying, *Verily every man living is altogether vanity* (Psalm 39:5). For is not eating and drinking, and caring for the things of the world, vanity? And yet this very vanity is useful: for it profits men born into the world, so that, exercised in their bodies by these things, they may learn the mystery of the Creator. For compared with things eternal they are vain; but in their own kind they are good, since they are necessary.

*(Verse 21.) But by reason of him who hath subjected the same in hope.* What the hope is he adds at once, saying, *Because the creature itself also shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of the children of God.* Since the creation cannot speak against its Maker, it was made subject because of Him, not without hope. For, set in labour, it has this comfort, that it will have rest when all have believed whom God knows will believe; for whose sake also He made it subject.

*(Verse 22.) For we know that the whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now.* To travail is to suffer pain. The sense is this: that the whole creation, through its daily labour, groans and suffers pain until now. "Until now" means for as long as it is being read. For the elements themselves perform their tasks with anxiety: for both sun and moon fulfil the courses appointed them not without labour; and the spirit of animals is straitened with great groaning to perform its services, for we see them groaning as they are forced unwilling to labour. All these, then, wait for rest, that they may be freed from servile work. But if this were a service that availed for winning God's favour, the creation would rejoice and not grieve; but because it has been made subject to the bondage of corruption for our sake, it grieves. For it sees its works perish daily: daily its work rises and sets. Rightly, then, does that grieve whose working belongs not to eternity but to corruption. So far, then, as we are given to understand, they are anxious enough about our salvation, knowing that it serves for their own liberation the sooner, if only we acknowledge the Author more quickly. Knowing this, then, let us show ourselves worthy with all care and diligence, so as to be an example to others as well, moved not only by pity for ourselves but also for the sake of the creation which groans, suffering wrongs day and night: for we are accustomed to keep watch more readily in the causes of others.

*(Verse 23.) And not only they, but ourselves also, which have the firstfruits of the Spirit, even we ourselves groan within ourselves, waiting for the redemption of our body.* After the creation of the world, he added that we too wait for this, and groan to God, that all who are destined for life may be set free from here: because for Christians this world is a sea. For as the sea, stirred up by contrary winds, leaps up and raises a storm for those who sail it, so the world, stirred by the conspiracy of the faithless, troubles the minds of the faithful. And the enemy does this with such variety that one does not know what to avoid first. For if the powers that be cease to be against us, he stirs up the minds of private men; and if they too are restrained, he kindles a fire through those of our own household; and if this is put down, by his art he makes discord between brothers themselves: so that the house, battered at its four corners, may fall in somewhere. For this reason Christians must with one mind flee from here: for they ought to follow the example of holy Simeon, who, knowing that a war is waged here against faithlessness, asked to be let depart in peace (Luke 2:29). This is what we each pray for all when we pray *Thy kingdom come* (Matthew 6:10): for then will be the redemption of our body, that is, of all Christians; for by "the body" he meant all, since we are members one of another. The Apostle, indeed, as a man of God, thinks well of us in saying that we groan to be set free from here, as Simeon groaned; but I see that we are held by the delight of the world. By what has been said above, then, he shows what force the creation suffers for our sake: when even we, whom its service profits, and who have the Spirit of God as our helper, groan to be set free from here, so as to obtain the promised rewards. How much more, then, does the creation groan, which neither has the Holy Spirit as helper nor labours in its own cause — and add to this that it sees the things it brings forth by its labour, so that they may serve as food for the servants of God, sacrificed against what is right to idols! So it grieves the more, and longs to be set free the sooner, knowing that this serves to insult the Creator.

*(Verse 24.) For we are saved by hope.* He says this because by hoping for what God promised in Christ, we have gained merit for ourselves, so as to be set free. So we have been set free in hope, because what is to come is nothing other than what we believe. *But hope that is seen is not hope.* It is plain that what is seen is not hope, but what is not seen: and so believers are to be rewarded, because they hope for what they do not see.

*(Verse 25.) For what a man seeth, why doth he yet hope for? But if we hope for that we see not, then do we with patience wait for it.* There is no doubt that things not seen are hoped for: for future things are waited for. This waiting is patience, which deserves much from God: that, waiting from day to day, one desires the kingdom of God to come, and does not doubt because it delays.

*(Verse 26.) Likewise the Spirit also helpeth our infirmities* in prayer. Since above he mentioned that we groan and pray for deliverance; and, set in pain, we want either the things that were said to be far off to be done at once, or the things through which we come to merit to be taken away quickly; and we seem to pray with a weak prayer when its effect is denied it. For that prayer is weak which asks against reason; and so he shows that this weakness is helped by the Holy Spirit who has been given us. And He helps because He does not allow to be done the things that are asked either before they ought to be asked, or that are contrary to us. *For we know not what we should pray for as we ought.* He has declared that the weakness of our prayer means ignorance: for we are deceived, thinking that what we ask for profits us, when it does not. So when the same Apostle prayed three times that his temptations should leave him, because they were frequent, the Lord said to him, *My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness* (2 Corinthians 12:9): that is, a man lays up merit in this way, when he is found patient in tribulations. He learned, then, that what he asked was against him. And sometimes a petition is proud and foolish, as was that of the two apostles James and John, to whom, asking for unfitting and boundless things, it was said, *Ye know not what ye ask* (Matthew 20:22). *But the Spirit itself maketh intercession for us with groanings which cannot be uttered.* The Spirit of the Lord is said to intercede for us not with human speech, but after the manner of His own nature. For when what is of God speaks to God, it must speak in the manner in which He speaks of whom it is. For no one speaks with his fellow-citizen in another tongue. For the Spirit given us pours Himself over our prayers, so as to cover our unskilfulness and lack of foresight with His own movement, and to ask of God for us the things that profit us.

*(Verse 27.) And he that searcheth the hearts knoweth what is the mind of the Spirit, because he maketh intercession for the saints according to the will of God.* It is plain that to God, from whom nothing is silent or hidden, the prayer of all spirits is known; how much more that of the Holy Spirit, who is certainly of the same substance, and who speaks not by a striking of the air, nor as angels do, nor as the rest of creatures, but as befits His Godhead! He speaks to God, then, when He seems to us to be silent; for He both sees, though He is not seen, and asks for the things which He knows please God and profit us. And the same Spirit puts Himself between for us especially then, when He knows that we ask for contrary things through ignorance, not through boasting.

*(Verse 28.) And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose.* He says this because, even if those who love God have prayed unskilfully, it will not harm them: for God, knowing the purpose of their heart and their slackness, does not count it against them that they ask for things hurtful to them, but grants the things that are to be given to those who love God. Hence the Lord too says, *Your Father knoweth what things ye have need of, before ye ask him* (Matthew 6:8). They, then, are called according to His purpose, whom God foreknew, as believers, would be fit for Him; so that they were known before they believed.

*(Verse 29.) For whom he did foreknow.* Those whom He foreknew would be devoted to Him, these He chose to lay hold of the promised rewards; so that those who seem to believe, and do not remain in the faith they began, are denied by God to be chosen: because those whom God has chosen remain with Him. For there is one who is chosen for a time, like Saul and Judas, not by foreknowledge but by present righteousness. *To be conformed to the image of his Son.* He says this because they are predestined to the world to come for this, that they may become like the Son of God, as I have already mentioned above. *That he might be the firstborn among many brethren.* Rightly firstborn, who was not made but born before all creation: after whose pattern God has deigned to adopt men as His sons. Christ is also firstborn in the regeneration. He is also the firstborn from the dead, whose nature is not known. And firstborn, ascending into heaven after His victory. He is called our brother, then, the firstborn in all things, because He deigned to be born a man; but He is Lord, because He is our God, as the prophet Jeremiah says, *This is our God, and there shall none other be accounted of in comparison of him* (Baruch 3:35).

*(Verse 30.) Moreover whom he did predestinate, them he also called.* To call is to help one who is thinking about faith, or to prick the heart of one whom He knows will listen. *And whom he called, them he also justified: and whom he justified, them he also glorified.* He says the same as above: that those whom God foreknew to be fit for Him remain believers, because it cannot be otherwise; and those whom God foreknew, He also justified; and so He also glorified them, that they might become like the Son of God. As for the rest, whom God did not foreknow, He has no care for them in this grace, because He did not foreknow that they would be fit. And if they believe, or are chosen for a time because they seem good — lest righteousness should seem to be despised — they do not remain so as to be glorified; like Judas Iscariot, or those seventy-two who, having been chosen, took offence and withdrew from the Saviour (John 6:66).

*(Verse 31.) What shall we then say to these things? If God be for us, who can be against us?* It is plain that, when God bears witness for us, who is there that would dare to accuse us, when He Himself who is the judge foreknew us beforehand and judged us fit?

*(Verse 32.) He that spared not his own Son, but delivered him up for us all.* He urges that we ought to be secure because of faith in this way: by showing that God, as foreknowing, gave His Son up to death for us before we had become faithful from ungodly men. *How shall he not with him also freely give us all things?* He says that God long ago decreed that those who believe in Christ should be rewarded. For if He bestowed on us what is greater and chief — to give up His true and dearest Son for us while we were still ungodly — why should He not be believed ready to do what is less for us who believe in Him? For the rewards are already prepared for believers: for it is less to give us all things with Him than to give Him up to death for our sake.

*(Verse 33.) Who shall lay any thing to the charge of God's elect?* It is plain that no one dares, or is able, to reopen God's judgment and foreknowledge concerning us. For who is there that could disapprove what God approves, when no one is equal to God? *It is God that justifieth* (Isaiah 50:8). This is in the prophet Isaiah, which he here sets down as though his own: because there is no other who could find fault with what God approves. Or might it be that God Himself accuses us? But He cannot accuse what He justifies.

*(Verse 34.) Who is he that condemneth? It is Christ that died, yea rather, that is risen again, who is even at the right hand of God, who also maketh intercession for us.* He denies that God accuses us, because He justifies us; and he says that Christ cannot condemn us, because He loves us with such love that He died for us, and, rising again, always pleads our cause with the Father. And His plea cannot be disregarded, because He is at the right hand of God, that is, in the honour that God has; so that, secure about God the Father and about Christ His Son, who will judge, we may rejoice in faith in Him. Hence is that which He says to the apostle Peter: *Behold, Satan hath desired to have you, that he may sift you as wheat: but I have prayed for thee, that thy faith fail not* (Luke 22:31–32). In this way, then, the Saviour makes intercession for us. For knowing the shamelessness and the loftiness of our adversary when he moves against us, if we do not consent to him, the Saviour makes intercession for us, that he may not presume anything violently against us, and that his insolence may be kept off. Yet the Son is said to make intercession for this reason — though He Himself does everything, and is equal to God the Father — that, since God is said to be one, the Father and the Son should not be thought a single one, or a union: for the distinction of the persons the Scripture speaks so, both to teach that the Son is not unequal, and to put the Father first, because He is the Father, and because all things are from Him.

*(Verse 35.) Who shall separate us from the love of Christ?* That is: who will be able to turn us away from the love of Christ, who has bestowed on us such great and countless benefits? *Shall tribulation?* But it does not do it, because no torments overcome the love of a firm Christian. For the love of one who loves is stirred up the more if he is forbidden to love one whose benefits he has felt; for while they think they are sowing into His deserts, they rouse the memory of His benefits.

*(Verses 35–37.) Or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or peril, or sword? As it is written, For thy sake we are killed all the day long; we are accounted as sheep for the slaughter. Nay, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him that loved us.* This is written in the forty-third psalm. It is plain, then, that all these things that he lists — ruin, tribulations, afflictions and death — cannot be set beside or compared with the love of Christ which He has sown in us. For we have His benefits, far greater than all these things which seem adverse. For if we die for Him — which seems the gravest of these — He too died for us; but He to profit us, whereas our death does not profit Him, but us. For we lose a temporal life, so that an eternal one may be paid back to us. And what wonder is it if servants die for a good Lord, when the Lord died for servants, and wicked ones? His benefits, then, prevail, and urge the mind to persevere for the sake of Him who loved us.

*(Verses 38–39.) For I am persuaded, that neither death* — this confidence comes from Christ's pledge, by which He promised to help in tribulation the faith dedicated to Him — *nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come, nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature, shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord.* These are all the things that are brought against us by the devil to lead us away, and he mentions them to fortify us: so that, if they come, trusting in hope and in the help of Christ, armed with faith, we may fight against him. For what if death is inflicted — is it not the greatest gain to find an occasion of going more quickly into the promised kingdom? Nor, if the present life is promised us furnished with rank, ought it to turn us away from the hope of the benefit of Christ, who, we know, will profit us not only in the future but in the present as well. Nor indeed, if an angel shows himself to us to seduce us, suborned by the deceits of his father the devil, ought he to prevail against us, since we know that nothing is to be set before Christ, as the Angel of great counsel (Isaiah 9:6). Nor, if a mighty work is done by anyone — as one is said to have been done by Simon Magus, who is said to have flown up in the air to make Christ's people stumble — ought it to lessen our faith, since we know that the Saviour, taken up by a cloud that served Him, ascended above all the heavens (Acts 1:9). Nor, if he shows himself to us in a height — of which the apostle John says, *the depths of Satan* (Revelation 2:24) — ought he to take us away from devotion to the Lord Jesus, who, we know, came down from heaven to join earthly things with spiritual. Nor, if by a phantasm, by which he plans to seduce, he shows us a depth wonderful for its horror, by which, terrified, we might perhaps give in to him — not even so is it right for us to break faith with Christ, who, we know, went down into the depth of the earth for our sake, and, death being crushed, set the human race free. Nor, if he pledges to us things to come, as he promised Eve (Genesis 3:4–5), shall we give him our assent, parted from Christ, whom we believe and know to be God in power and in nature. Nor, if by the art and subtlety of his cunning he fashions another creature for an hour, as Jannes and Jambres fashioned them before Pharaoh, is it fitting that by these things he should call us back from the true God the Creator, who, as we well know, founded through Christ His Son a creation that exists for ever (2 Timothy 3:8). Some think he spoke of idols as "another creature"; but this is not true: for he must have meant the one which Satan is seen to fashion, to the wonder of those he seduces. For which of the faithful would be seduced to those things from which he withdrew when the error had been made plain? But Satan plans and fashions things by which he may seduce even the elect. There is nothing, then, that can separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus: for God has shown us His love in Christ, in giving Him up for us.
