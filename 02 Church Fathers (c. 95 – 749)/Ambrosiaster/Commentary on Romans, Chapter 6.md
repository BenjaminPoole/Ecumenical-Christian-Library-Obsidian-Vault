# Commentary on Romans, Chapter 6

*While almost all of the works in this vault use Victorian translations, due to the English translations of this commentary being in copyright, it was machine-translated (by Claude, 2026) from the public-domain Latin. It has not been checked by a human translator.*

*Commentary on Paul's Epistle to the Romans, long printed among the works of Ambrose. Its author, whom Erasmus first called "Ambrosiaster", wrote at Rome while Damasus was bishop there (366–384). Chapter 6 of the Epistle; the verse numbers are the Benedictine editors'.*

*(Verse 1.) What shall we say then? Shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound? God forbid.* That is: must we always sin, so that the gift of God, covering our sins, may abound — so that, always hoping for the forgiveness of sins because God is kind, we may make much of the grace of Christ? Certainly not. For God had mercy on us through Christ for this purpose, that, sinning no more for the future, we may make both merit for ourselves and the grace of God to reign in us. For he who goes back again to the old man — that is, to the manner of his past life — takes away the kingdom of God's grace and hands it over to sin. For we received mercy for two reasons: that the kingdom of the devil should be shut out, and that the rule of God should be preached to those who did not know it; and by this very thing honour has been won for us.

*(Verse 2.) How shall we, that are dead to sin, live any longer therein?* He says this because, while we lived to sin, we were dead before God: for he who sins lives to sin, as he who does not sin lives to God. But when the grace of God came upon us through Christ, and the spiritual washing regenerated us through faith, we began to live to God and to be dead to sin, which is the devil. To die to sin, though, is this: to be set free from sin, and to become a servant of God. So, being now dead to sin, let us not go back to our former evils; lest we live again to sin, and, dying to God and losing our honour, incur the punishment we have escaped.

*(Verse 3.) Know ye not, that so many of us as were baptized into Jesus Christ were baptized into his death?* He says this so that we may know that, being baptized, we ought no longer to sin: because when we are baptized we die with Christ. This is to be baptized into His death: for there all our sins die, so that, made new, with death laid aside, we may be seen to rise again, born anew, to life; so that, as Christ died to sin and rose again, we too may have through baptism the hope of resurrection. Baptism, then, is the death of sin, so that another birth may follow, which, while the frame of the body remains, renews the man in mind, the oldness of all evil deeds being buried.

*(Verse 4.) Therefore we are buried with him by baptism into death: that like as Christ was raised up from the dead by the glory of the Father, even so we also should walk in newness of life.* In saying this he points also to what comes first: that Christ raised His own body from the dead — for He is Himself the power of God the Father — as He says, *Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up. But he spake*, it says, *of the temple of his body* (John 2:19, 21); and that He rose into a new life, which knows death no more. It is called new because it was given by Christ: it is the Christian discipline. And we, being baptized, are buried with Christ (Colossians 2:12), so that for the future we may follow this life in which Christ rose again. Baptism, then, is the pledge and the image of the resurrection; so that, abiding now in Christ's commandments, we may not roll back again to the things that are past. For he who dies sins no more: for death is the end of sin. And it is celebrated with water for this reason: that, as water washes away the filth of the body, so we may believe that through baptism we are spiritually cleansed of all sin and made new; so that what is incorporeal may be washed invisibly.

*(Verse 5.) For if we have been planted together in the likeness of his death, we shall be also in the likeness of his resurrection.* He says that we can rise happily then, if we have been planted together in the likeness of His death: that is, if, laying aside all vices in baptism and carried over into a new life, we sin no more for the future. By this we shall also be like Him in His resurrection: for the likeness of the death will bring about a like resurrection. The apostle John mentions this in his epistle, saying, *We know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him* (1 John 3:2): that is, rising immortal and glorious. Yet this likeness will not be such that there is no difference: for it will be a likeness in the glory of the body, not in the nature of the Godhead.

*(Verses 6–7.) Knowing this, that our old man is crucified with him, that the body of sin might be destroyed, that henceforth we should not serve sin.* He weaves many things together and repeats them, to teach the baptized that they ought no longer to sin — above all, that they should not go back to idolatry, for that is the gravest crime and the head of all error — lest they lose the grace they have received from God through Christ. By "the old man", then, he means their past deeds: for as a man is called new through faith and a pure life, so he is called old through unbelief and evil deeds. These deeds he says are crucified, that is, dead, so that the body of sin may be destroyed, which is all crimes together. For he calls all sins together a body, which he says is destroyed by a good life and the catholic faith.

*(Verse 8.) Now if we be dead with Christ, we believe that we shall also live with him* (Galatians 5:24). It is plain that those who have crucified the flesh — that is, the world with its vices and lusts — die to the world and die with Christ, and are conformed to the eternal and saving life; so that they may deserve to be made like the glory of Christ. And the flesh, that is, the body, is crucified in this way: if its desires are trodden down, which, while sin remains in it, are produced from the transgression begun by the first man. For in the flesh the devil is crucified, since it is he who deceives through the flesh. "Flesh", then, is understood sometimes as the world, that is, the elements; sometimes as the body of man; or as the soul itself, when it follows the vices of the body.

*(Verses 9–10.) Knowing that Christ being raised from the dead dieth no more; death hath no more dominion over him. For in that he died, he died unto sin once: but in that he liveth, he liveth unto God.* He shows the security of eternity in the Saviour's resurrection, which it is given us to reach if we strive after a better life. For he who lives to God by doing well truly lives, because he has eternal life.

*(Verse 11.) Likewise reckon ye also yourselves to be dead indeed unto sin, but alive unto God through Jesus Christ our Lord.* He speaks so as to teach that one cannot die to sin and live to God in any other way than by placing all hope in Christ; whom he calls our Lord, so that nothing of the Law may avail to take hold of salvation in the world to come, when one lives to God in no other way than through Christ.

*(Verse 12.) Let not sin therefore reign in your mortal body, that ye should obey it in the lusts thereof.* The body is mortal because of Adam's transgression, but by faith in Christ it is believed that it will be immortal. That one may come to this promise, he says that sin must not be obeyed, lest it reign in our mortal body; and it reigns when it commands. But if it does not reign, this body will not be seen to be mortal, because it is in the hope of eternity. He did not call the body mortal because of its dissolution, but because of the punishment of hell: so that he is called mortal who is to be sent into hell; because those who obey sin do not escape the second death, of the underworld, from which the Saviour has set free those who believe in Him. In saying "mortal body", then, he meant the whole man; because those who obey sin will be called mortal. For *the soul*, it says, *that sinneth, it shall die* (Ezekiel 18:4) — that is, the whole man; for no one will be judged without his body.

*(Verse 13.) Neither yield ye your members as instruments of unrighteousness unto sin.* He shows that the devil attacks us with our own darts: for an occasion is given him through our sins; so that, when God forsakes us, he receives power to mock us and put us to death. So we ought to keep our members from every work of unrighteousness, so that our enemy, found unarmed, may be brought under. And he did not say "yield your bodies", but "your members": for it is through the members as servants that men go wrong, not through the whole body. *But yield yourselves unto God, as those that are alive from the dead.* By death he means ignorance and unbelief, together with an evil life: because life is to know God through Christ. For since no one takes the beginning of life without a parent, and all things have taken their beginning from God unto life through Christ, therefore he who does not know that God is the parent of all through Christ is not said to have life; that is, he is reckoned as though he did not exist: for he denies his own existence when he thinks he exists without God as his parent. So ignorance and a shameful life are death: for these vices gain death — not this death which is common to all, but the death of hell, as I mentioned above. So that the knowledge of God our parent, and holy living, are life — not this life which is subject to its setting, but that of the world to come, which is called eternal. Therefore, he says, *yield yourselves unto God*, in knowing whom you have gone forward to salvation; so that, renouncing a shameful life, you might be alive from the dead. *And your members as instruments of righteousness unto God.* He wishes us to govern our conduct with such modesty that our actions may advance the righteousness of God — not the righteousness of the world, for the righteousness of the world is without faith in Christ, without which there is not life but death — and that we may furnish it with arms to defend us. For when we furnish it with arms by good works, we make ourselves worthy to be helped by it; because the righteousness of God despises the unworthy. For where the righteousness of God is, there the Holy Spirit dwells to help our weakness. For as we furnish arms to sin when we do evil, so we furnish them to righteousness when we live rightly, keeping our members from all shamefulness.

*(Verse 14.) For sin shall not have dominion over you: for ye are not under the Law, but under grace.* He says that, if we walk according to the commandments He gives, sin cannot have dominion over us; for it has dominion over those who sin. For if we do not walk as He commands, we are under the Law; but if we do not sin, we are not under the Law, but under grace. But if we sin, we turn back under the Law, and sin will begin to have dominion over us; because *whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin* (John 8:34). And he must be under the Law so long as he has not received forgiveness: for sin, by the authority of the Law, makes the sinner guilty. And so he to whom forgiveness has been given, and who guards himself from sinning for the future — over him sin will not have dominion, nor is he under the Law. For the authority of the Law ceases in regard to him who has been set free from sin: for those whom the law holds guilty have been handed over to it by sin. He, then, who has been stripped of his sins cannot be under the Law.

*(Verse 15.) What then? shall we sin, because we are not under the Law, but under grace? God forbid.* Since the Law is from God, he put this question to himself, lest perhaps it should be raised against him: "If the Law is from God", he says, "why ought we not to be under the Law?" He clears this up, and teaches that it is by the will of God, the author of the Law, that we have been set free by Christ from under the law. For though the Law was rightly given — for it was given both to show guilty before God those who had sinned before the Law, and to be a cause of fear, so that for the future men should not sin — yet because the human race, through the feebleness of its weakness, could not restrain itself from sins, and became liable to the death of the underworld, God, moved by the goodness of His mercy, by which He always comes to the help of the human race, provided through Christ the means by which a remedy should now be given to those in despair: so that, having received the forgiveness of sins, rescued from the Law which held them liable, and relieved and restored, they might, with God's help through faith, resist the vices by which before they had been subdued. And so we have not sinned as men who despise the Law of God, but have followed, as it were, the providence of God Himself, through Christ.

*(Verse 16.) Know ye not, that to whom ye yield yourselves servants to obey, his servants ye are to whom ye obey; whether of sin unto death, or of obedience unto righteousness?* Now, lest we profess one thing and do another, and, while we are called servants of God, be found by our deeds servants of the devil, he warns us beforehand and declares that we are the servants of him whose will we carry out in our works; and that it does no little harm to confess God as Lord while serving the devil in our acts. For God long ago marked and accused this, saying, *This people honoureth me with their lips, but have removed their heart far from me* (Isaiah 29:13). And the Lord says in the Gospel, *No man can serve two masters* (Matthew 6:24). And in the law it is said, *God is not mocked* (Galatians 6:7).

*(Verses 17–18.) But God be thanked, that ye were the servants of sin, but ye have obeyed from the heart that form of doctrine which was delivered you. Being then made free from sin, ye became the servants of righteousness.* Believing in Christ, we have become servants of righteousness. For we are called the servants of him whom we obey. And because it is righteous to obey Christ — for He is Himself righteousness, and what He commands is righteous — he says that we have become servants of righteousness from the heart, not from the law; from the will, not from fear: so that our profession may come forth from the judgment of the mind. For we have been led to faith by nature, not by the law; and in this form of doctrine we were made by the command of God, who fashioned nature. For we have it in our nature to know from whom, and through whom, and in whom we were created. The form of doctrine, then, is that into which the Creator delivered us by nature; this is what he said above, *They are a law unto themselves* (Romans 2:14): since they see that what they believe belongs to their nature; so that what the law and the prophets preached to the Jews about Christ, the Gentiles trust in from the heart. Hence he gives thanks to the Lord that, when we were servants of sin, we obeyed from the heart, believing in Christ, so as to serve God, not through the law of Moses, but through the law of nature.

*(Verse 19.) I speak after the manner of men because of the infirmity of your flesh.* In mentioning the weakness of the flesh, he shows that he asks less of man than is due in the worship of God. And so it follows: *For as ye have yielded your members servants to uncleanness and to iniquity unto iniquity; even so now yield your members servants to righteousness unto holiness.* To take from us any occasion of fearing to come to the faith — as though it might seem unbearable and harsh to us — he has commanded us to serve God in the same measure in which we formerly served the devil. Yet God ought surely to be served more readily than the devil, since here salvation is worked, there damnation. But the spiritual physician asks no more of us than this, lest, considering our weakness, we should flee from the commandments as heavy, and remain in death. Hence the Lord says, *Take my yoke upon you … for my yoke is easy, and my burden is light* (Matthew 11:29–30).

*(Verses 20–21.) For when ye were the servants of sin, ye were free from righteousness. What fruit had ye then in those things whereof ye are now ashamed? for the end of those things is death.* It is plain that he who is free from God is the servant of sin: for while he sins he withdraws from God and comes under sin. What, then, are the fruits of sin? Fruits in which we once lived, and at which, learning a good life, we are put to shame, because we lived shamefully before. And not only this, but the creed of the pagans too is foul and filthy, above all that which was devised in Phrygia, in which, unless an unclean man is present, the rite is dumb and the religion grows numb! Behold a liberty full of stains and bound up with filth, whose work has a shameful wage: *the end of which is death.* By "end" he meant the going out of life and of deeds, upon which follows either death or life. But in this place the death is twofold: for one passes from death to death.

*(Verse 22.) But now being made free from sin, and become servants to God, ye have your fruit unto holiness, and the end everlasting life.* That is, if, having received the forgiveness of sins, we strive after good deeds, we shall gain holiness, and we shall have as our end, that is, our going out, everlasting life: for from this death, which he called the end, we shall pass to a life which is without end.

*(Verse 23.) For the wages of sin is death.* He calls death the gain of sin, because death is through sin. And so those who keep themselves from sins for the future receive eternal life as their wages: because those who do not sin are strangers to the second death. *But the gift of God is eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord.* For as those who follow sin gain death, so those who follow the grace of God — that is, faith in Christ, which forgives sins — will have eternal life; so that they rejoice to be dissolved for a time, knowing that they will obtain this life, which is free from all weariness and has no succession. Longing for it, holy Simeon asked to be dissolved from here (Luke 2:29), that he might depart in peace, that is, into life, which suffers no disquiet. And he declares that this gift has been given us by God through Christ our Lord, so that we may give thanks to God the Father through no other than through His Son.
