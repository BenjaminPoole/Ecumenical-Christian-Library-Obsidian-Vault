# Commentary on Romans, Chapter 7

*While almost all of the works in this vault use Victorian translations, due to the English translations of this commentary being in copyright, it was machine-translated (by Claude, 2026) from the public-domain Latin. It has not been checked by a human translator.*

*Commentary on Paul's Epistle to the Romans, long printed among the works of Ambrose. Its author, whom Erasmus first called "Ambrosiaster", wrote at Rome while Damasus was bishop there (366–384). Chapter 7 of the Epistle; the verse numbers are the Benedictine editors'.*

*(Verse 1.) Know ye not, brethren, (for I speak to them that know the law,)* To strengthen their minds in the divine teaching, he uses the example of human law: so as again to persuade them of heavenly things by earthly ones, as God too is known from the creation of the world. For since the whole belongs to One, though things are different, yet in some part they are like one another. The Romans, then, know law, since they are not barbarians, but have grasped natural justice partly of themselves and partly from the Greeks: for the Romans' laws were brought from Athens, as the Greeks' were from the Hebrews. For before Moses the Law was not unknown; but it had no order and no authority. He says, then, to those who are not ignorant of law: *how that the law hath dominion over a man as long as he liveth.* It is no secret that the whole life of man is under the law of nature, which was given to the world: this law is general. But now he sets out another, a special one — though it too is general, yet since it is not received by all, it becomes special — through which he wishes to prove his assertion; for he wishes to hand on the truth step by step. He says, then:

*(Verse 2.) For the woman which hath an husband is bound by the law to her husband so long as he liveth.* This law is from the Gospel, not from Moses, nor from earthly justice. Learning something, then, with nature as their guide, and something from the law of Moses, they have been made perfect through the Gospel of Christ. By the following example, then, he persuades them more plainly that Christianity has been stripped of the law of deeds, though not of all law; and that it is no longer good to be under the law, lest one who goes back under the law make void the grace of God: because the grace of God has set man free from the law, that he may serve God in the spirit, that is, in the mind. For as a woman, when her husband is dead, is freed from the law of her husband, though not from the law of nature, so too those who by the grace of God have been freed from the law by which they were held guilty — the law is dead to them, so that, being joined to Christianity, they are not adulterers. For if the law lives with them, they are adulterers, and it will profit them nothing to be called Christians, since they will be liable to punishment. For he will be an adulterer not against the law but against the Gospel, who, the law being dead and he joined to the Gospel, afterwards goes back to the law. For the law is said to be dead when it no longer knows the man whom it held guilty, the law's authority having ceased.

*(Verse 3.) But if her husband be dead, she is free from that law; so that she is no adulteress, though she be married to another man.* Those who have been taken away from the Law, have received forgiveness of sins, and been joined to the Gospel, are not adulterers against the Law, because it is dead with them. But if, while keeping the Law, they think they are coming to the Gospel, they will be guilty and adulterers: because they have joined themselves to the faith while the law is alive, and they will be adulterers towards both.

*(Verse 4.) Wherefore, my brethren, ye also are become dead to the law by the body of Christ.* Since our Saviour allowed the devil to crucify His body, knowing that this was for us and against him, he says that we have been saved through the body of Christ. For to die to the law is to live to God, because the Law has dominion over sinners. He, then, whose sins are forgiven dies to the Law — that is, is set free from the Law. So through the body of Christ we obtain this benefit: for the Saviour, giving up His body, conquered death and condemned sin. For the devil sinned in Him when he killed Him, an innocent man who knew no sin at all. For while he was claiming man for himself because of sin, he was found guilty in the very one he was accusing. Hence it came about that all who believe in Christ are rescued from the law, sin being taken away: because sin, which is the devil, having been conquered through the body of Christ, has no authority over those who belong to Him by whom it was conquered. For since He does not sin, and is killed as though guilty, He conquered sin by sin; that is, He condemned the devil by his own sin, which he committed against Him; and He blotted out the handwriting which had been decreed by Adam's sin (Colossians 2:14), when He rose from the underworld, giving this pattern to those who believe in Him, that they cannot be held by the second death. And so we have become dead to the Law through the body of Christ: for he who has not died to the Law is guilty, and he who is guilty cannot escape the second death. *That ye should be married to another, even to him who is raised from the dead, that we should bring forth fruit unto God.* He says this because we have died to the Law for this purpose, that for the future we should be the servants of Christ alone: and this will be to bring forth fruit to God. For he is gained for God who remains in His grace, worthy of the promised resurrection.

*(Verse 5.) For when we were in the flesh.* Though he is in the flesh — for he is in the body — he denies that he is in the flesh: because a man is said to be in the flesh who follows something that is forbidden by the law. Being in the flesh, then, is understood in many ways. For every unbeliever is in the flesh, that is, carnal; and a Christian living under the law is in the flesh; and he who hopes for anything from men is in the flesh; and he who understands Christ amiss is in the flesh; and if any Christian has a dissolute life, he is in the flesh. In this place, though, we understand being in the flesh thus: that before faith we were in the flesh; for we lived under sin; that is, following carnal understanding, we lay subject to vices and sins. And the understanding of the flesh is not to believe spiritual things: that is, that a virgin gave birth without union with a man, and that a man is born again of water and the Holy Spirit, and that the soul, loosed from its bond with the flesh, rises again in the flesh. He who distrusts these things is in the flesh. Hence he says: *The motions of sins, which were by the law, did work in our members to bring forth fruit unto death.* It is plain that he who does not believe lives under sin, and is dragged captive to commit vices, so as to bear fruit for the second death: for death makes gain when men sin. Yet he says that the vices work in the members, not in the body, lest it give an occasion to those who handle the body badly: for in evil speaking the tongue is reproved, and in theft the hand is rebuked, and in one who turns a deaf ear the ears are blamed, and so in the rest. Though these come out of the heart, as the Lord testifies in the Gospel (Matthew 15:18), yet they are carried out in deeds by the service of the members. This argument touches the Jews, and those who are called Christians and wish to live under the Law; so that, learning that they are carnal, they may withdraw from the Law. Yet he shows that the motions of sins, which he says have dominion over those who live in the flesh, are shown by the Law, not made by the Law: for the Law is the informer of sin, not its mother, making sinners guilty.

*(Verse 6.) But now we are delivered from the law of death, wherein we were held.* We have been loosed from the Law, since we received the forgiveness of sins: for it no longer has power over us, since it has dominion over the faithless and over sinners. It is called the law of death because it punishes the guilty; it puts sinners to death. So it is not evil, but just. For though what the Law inflicts is an evil to those who suffer it, the Law is not evil, because it inflicts wrath justly. So to sinners it is not evil but just, and to the good it is spiritual. For who would doubt that it is a spiritual thing to forbid sinning? But because it could not do this — save man by forgiving sins — the law of faith was given, to set believers free from the dominion of the Law; so that those whom the Law held guilty unto death might be able to restore themselves to life. For to those it is the law of death, for whom it works wrath because of sin. *That we should serve in newness of spirit, and not in the oldness of the letter.* Here he is thought to touch the law, which he does make inferior by comparison with the law of faith, but does not judge to be hostile. For he says that we have been rescued from the law of death so that we may serve the law of faith, which has brought us a saving defence, which the old law could not; lest we should seem to make void the benefit of grace by serving the old law. "The old law" is certainly not the name of a crime, but of time, or age: it has grown old because it has ceased. The law of the spirit is the law of faith itself: for faith is in the mind, and is not learned by works, but believed with the heart; and the mind itself understands that what it believes belongs to its nature; it is not seen with the eyes, nor felt with the hands; and the gifts that are hoped for are not visible, nor earthly, but spiritual. The old law, then, was shaped on tables of stone; but the law of the spirit is written spiritually on the tables of the heart, so that it may be eternal; while the letter of the old law is worn away by age. There is also another interpretation of the law of the spirit: that, because the former law forbade the evil deed, while this one says that one must not sin even in the heart, it is called the law of the spirit, so as to make the whole man spiritual.

*(Verse 7.) What shall we say then? Is the law sin? God forbid.* Since he calls it the law of death, and rejoices that we have been rescued from it, and denies that God is served in it, he clears this up, lest he should be thought to call it unjust, when he says, *God forbid. Nay, I had not known sin, but by the law.* The Law, then, is not sin, but the informer of sin: for it showed both the sins that lay hidden, and that they would not go unpunished before God. When he had learned this, man was made guilty; and so he does not seem to give thanks to the Law. For who would gladly draw near to one who announces that punishment hangs over him? But he gives thanks to the law of faith: because he who was made guilty through the law of Moses has been reconciled to God through the law of faith. Yet that law too is just and good: for it is a good thing to point out an impending danger. But he gives thanks rather to Him through whom he lives, rescued from the danger. *For I had not known lust, except the law had said* in Exodus, *Thou shalt not covet* (Exodus 20:17). He did not set this lust apart from sin, but joined them, meaning this: that, when there was not even a suspicion that it was unlawful before God, "I came to know," he says, "that it is sin." Under his own person he pleads, as it were, a general case. So the Law forbids lust, which, because it is a delight, was not thought to be sin. For to covet something of one's neighbour seemed a simple matter; but the Law made it known that this is sin. For to worldly men nothing seems so unpunished and so pleasant as pleasure.

*(Verse 8.) But sin, taking occasion by the commandment, wrought in me all manner of concupiscence.* In saying *all manner of concupiscence* he means all sins. For above he mentions lust according to the Law; and now, adding the other vices to it, he said that all lust was wrought in man at the urging of the devil, whom he means by "sin"; so that the Law, which had been given to help man, became the opposite to him. For the devil, seeing help provided for man through the Law — man whom he rejoiced to hold in his condition, both because of Adam's sin and because of his own — understood that it had been made against himself. For he held it certain that one whom he saw placed under the Law had been taken from his dominion: for man had learned how to escape the punishment of the underworld. Hence his anger blazed up against man, to turn the Law upside down for him: so that, by doing forbidden things, he should offend God again and fall once more into the devil's power. He began, not to command, but to deceive subtly; since, when the Law was given, the devil lost his dominion, knowing that man belonged for the future to the judgment of God. *For without the law sin was dead.* This is to be understood in two ways: so that you may know that by "sin" is meant both the devil and the very thing that is called sin. For the devil is said to have been dead because before the Law he ceased from mocking man, and was, as it were, at rest: for he was secure about possessing him. And he said that sin was dead because it was thought not to be imputed before God: so with man it was dead, as though one could sin with impunity. For sin was not hidden, as I said above; but this was not known, that God would judge. When this was made plain by the giving of the Law, sin revived. Why did it revive, unless because it had lived before, and afterwards, through the sloth of men, was reckoned dead while it was alive? For sin was thought not to be imputed, when it was imputed. So it was held to be dead while it was living.

*(Verse 9.) For I was alive without the law once.* What is "without the Law", when there always was a Law, as I mentioned above, but that man lived without the fear of God, secure that God would not judge human deeds? *But when the commandment came, sin revived.* He said that sin revived to show that it had lived before; but afterwards, through the sloth of men, it was reckoned dead while it lived — as a false report is often spread about strangers. He shows, then, that at the beginning it was not unknown that sin is imputed before God — that is, that it was alive; but when the habit of sinning had blotted this out, it was reckoned dead, so that the crime was thought to fail with the person. But when the Law was given, or renewed, it revived with those with whom it had been reckoned dead: for they began to know that sin is imputed.

*(Verse 10.) And I died.* Man died, because he sees himself guilty before God, who before thought he would not be liable for the sins he committed. *And the commandment, which was ordained to life, I found to be unto death.* It is true that the Law was given for life; but when it made man guilty as a sinner not only for the past but for the future as well, the Law given for life came to be unto death — but, as I said, to the sinner: for to those who obey it, it profits unto life.

*(Verse 11.) For sin, taking occasion by the commandment, deceived me, and by it slew me.* In this place understand by "sin" the devil, who is the author of sin. He found an occasion through the law by which to glut his cruelty with the death of man: so that, since the Law threatens sinners, man, by his prompting, always committing what was forbidden, should offend God and incur the vengeance of the Law; so as to be condemned by that which had been given to help him. For since the Law was given against the devil's will, his envy blazed up against man, to stain him the more with vicious pleasures, lest he should escape from his hands.

*(Verse 12.) Wherefore the law is holy, and the commandment holy, and just, and good.* That no hostile suspicion should remain against the Law, he so commends it as to declare it not only just but also holy and good. That "commandment" means the Law is witnessed by the words of the Gospel; for it says, *If thou wilt enter into life, keep the commandments* (Matthew 19:17).

*(Verse 13.) Was then that which is good made death unto me? God forbid.* God forbid indeed: for how can it be that what is proved to be good should be understood to be death? *But sin, that it might appear sin, working death in me by that which is good.* He says that the devil took occasion through what is good to work evil for man, seducing him to death. For the Law was given, set out in writing, so that the evil things done before the Law through the allurement of desire might be proved to be sins, and be avoided for the future. Then Satan was set ablaze — by what is good, of course: for he saw that provision was being made for man, and urged him to unlawful things, so that instead of life from the Law, death should come to him. This is indeed to be laid to man's negligence, who so weakened the vigour of his nature by the desire of sinning that he could not beat back the suggestions of the adversary. Yet the enemy, whom he means by "sin", so that he might appear to be an enemy, took occasion through the Law to work death for man: for in urging him to unlawful things, he appears to be an enemy. Though he contrived death for man before the law as well — apart from the first cause, Adam — yet after the Law he devised greater punishments for him in the underworld, where the second death is. For it is less of a crime to have sinned before the Law was made known than after the Law. *That sin by the commandment might become exceeding sinful* (Genesis 15:16). What is "beyond measure", as though a measure had been set for offenders, when it is not lawful to sin? But they are the words of God, who says, *The iniquity of the Amorites is not yet full* (ibid.). By this He shows that there is a certain measure of offences, which, when sinners have filled it, they are judged not worthy of life at all. So too with Pharaoh: because he had filled up this measure, God's mighty works and signs were shown against him, by which the rest, made afraid, might exert themselves for the gains of life, so that life might be learned from one who died. But it is another measure that the Apostle is treating: for, to show that there is more sin under the Law than there had been before the Law, he means that, beyond this measure of sins, a larger measure grew up after the Law through the envy and cunning of Satan; so that the providence of God became contrary to man, when through fear of the Law he ought not to have sinned at all. So God, to overcome the devil's envy and to make His providence sure for man, changed the order, sending Christ the Saviour, who should both overcome the devil and confirm God's decrees concerning man.

*(Verse 14.) For we know that the law is spiritual.* Since he speaks to those who know the Law, he says, *We know that the law is spiritual*: for they would not put themselves under the Law unless they acknowledged it to be spiritual. He calls the Law of Moses, which was given on tables, spiritual: and it is spiritual because it forbids sinning, since it forbids the worship of things visible and carnal. This serves to commend the Law, lest it should perhaps be thought by anyone to deserve blame because it exercised severity against sinners. *But I am carnal, sold under sin.* He calls man carnal while he sins. *Sold under sin*: to be sold under sin is this — to take one's origin from Adam, who sinned first, and to be made subject to sin by one's own offence; as Isaiah the prophet says, *For your iniquities have ye sold yourselves* (Isaiah 50:1). For Adam sold himself first, and so all his seed is subject to sin. For this reason man is weak for keeping the commandments of the law unless he is fortified by divine help; and this is why he says, *The law is spiritual: but I am carnal, sold under sin*: that is, the Law is firm and just and without blame, but man is frail and brought under the yoke of his father's offence or his own, so that he cannot use his own power in obeying the Law. So he must take refuge in God's mercy, that he may escape the severity of the Law, and, unburdened of his offences, resist the enemy for the future with God's favour. For what is it to be subject to sin, but to have a body corrupted by the vice of the soul, into which sin may insert itself, and drive man like a captive to offences, so that he does its will? Hence the Lord says among other things, *Then cometh the devil, and taketh away the word out of their hearts, lest they should believe and be saved* (Luke 8:12). Hence the same Apostle says in another epistle, *We wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against spiritual wickedness in high places* (Ephesians 6:12) — who, as is well known, are the attendants of Satan. For before man's transgression, before he gave himself over to death, they had no power to come to the inward parts of man and to put hostile thoughts into him. That is why by his cunning it came about that he got round the man by conversing with him through the serpent (Genesis 3:1). But after he had got round him and brought him under the yoke, he received power over him to knock at the inner man, joining himself to his mind; so that a man cannot tell what in his thought is his own and what is the devil's, unless he looks to the law.

*(Verse 15.) For that which I do I allow not* — that is, "I do not understand". He does not understand what he does, because he sees that he knows one thing through the Law and does another. *For what I would, that do I not; but what I hate, that do I.* One who is subject to sin does, of course, what he does not wish.

*(Verse 16.) If then I do that which I would not, I consent unto the law that it is good.* He proves that the Law rightly forbids, when he declares that he does against his will what the law forbids; and he says that what the Law commands belongs to his nature, since he says that what he does outside it is hateful to him.

*(Verse 17.) Now then it is no more I that do it, but sin that dwelleth in me.* For he sees that what he does is forbidden by the law, and agrees that it ought not to be done; and so he declares that there is another, at whose urging these things are done, that is, sin. And he always names it "sin", though it is known to be the devil with his angels, because, unless the first man had sinned, these things would not have come about: and so it is sin that does all these things.

*(Verse 18.) For I know that in me (that is, in my flesh,) dwelleth no good thing.* He does not say, as some think, that the flesh is evil, but that what dwells in the flesh is not good, but sin. How does sin dwell in the flesh, since it is not a substance, but the transgression of good? Because the body of the first man was corrupted through sin, so that it could be dissolved; the very corruption of sin remains in the body by the condition of the offence, holding the force of the divine sentence given against Adam, which is the seal of the devil, at whose prompting he sinned. By this, then, which remains on account of the deed, sin is said to dwell in the flesh, to which the devil comes as though to his own law; and sin remains as it were in sin, because the flesh is now of sin; so that he may deceive man with evil suggestions, lest man do what the law commands. *For to will is present with me.* He declares the things the Law commands to be so good that he says they please him by nature, and that he wishes to do them. *But how to perform that which is good I find not.* What the Law commands pleases him, then, and there is the will to do it; but power and strength to fulfil it are wanting: because he is so weighed down by the power of sin that he cannot go where he will, nor is he able to speak against it, since another is lord of his power. For man is now weighed down by the habit of sinning, and more readily gives way to sin than to the law, which he knows teaches good things; and if he wishes to do good, habit presses on him, with the enemy as its helper.

*(Verse 19.) For the good that I would I do not: but the evil which I would not, that I do.* He repeats it often, to make it clear. This, then, is what was said above: that, like a captive, for the reasons given above, he is compelled to do what sin wills.

*(Verse 20.) Now if I do that I would not, it is no more I that do it, but sin that dwelleth in me.* This is what he has already mentioned above: that, oppressed and brought under the yoke of sin, he carries out not his own will but sin's. Because he says that man sins against his will, must he be thought free from blame, since he does what he does not wish, weighed down by the force of a power? Certainly not: for these things were begun by his own fault and sloth. For since he gave himself over to sin by consenting, sin rules him by right: for first it persuades, so as afterwards to rule him when he has been overcome. But the Apostle sets out these things to exalt the grace of God: from how great evils it has set man free, what ruin he draws from Adam, and what benefits he has obtained through Christ; to show to whom not even the Law could bring help.

*(Verse 21.) I find then a law, that, when I would do good, evil is present with me.* He declares that the Law of Moses gives its consent to his will, against the sin that dwells in the flesh, which compels him to do something other than what the man and the Law wish.

*(Verse 22.) For I delight in the law of God after the inward man.* He says that the mind delights in the things that are taught by the Law: this is the inward man. For sin does not dwell in the mind, but in the flesh, which comes from the origin of the flesh of sin; and through propagation all flesh becomes flesh of sin. For if the soul too were from propagation, sin would dwell in it as well: for Adam's soul sinned more than his body; but the sin of the soul corrupted the body. In the flesh, then, sin dwells, as it were at the doors of the soul, so as not to let it go where it will. But if it dwelt in the soul, man would never know himself; whereas now he knows himself, and delights in the law of God.

*(Verse 23.) But I see another law in my members, warring against the law of my mind, and bringing me into captivity to the law of sin which is in my members.* He mentions two laws, of which he insists that he sees one in his members, that is, in the outward man, which is the flesh or body. This is the hostile law; this fights against the mind, dragging it captive into the condition of sin, lest, entering any further, it should find a deliverer. The other is the law of the mind, which is the law of Moses, or the natural law, which is in the mind. This is oppressed by the violence of sin — through its own negligence, indeed: for in loving vices it submits itself to sin, so as to be held captive by habit itself; for habit lords it over man. He names, indeed, four laws: first the spiritual law, which is also the natural law, which was renewed through Moses and set up with authority — this is the Law of God; next, the law of the mind, consenting to the law of God; third, the law of sin, which he says dwells in the members from the transgression of the first man; and fourth, that which is seen in the members, when it suggests evil things and withdraws. But these laws, being repeated, seem to be four, though they are two, that is, good and evil. For the law of the mind is the same as the spiritual law, or the law of Moses, which is called the law of God; and the law of sin is the same as that which he says is seen in the members, which speaks against the law of our mind.

*(Verses 24–25.) O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord.* He calls man wretched because he is born under sin. Is not man truly wretched, who has succeeded to this inheritance of transgression, having sin as an enemy with him, through which Satan has access to him? For Adam built steps by which the despoiler climbs up to his sons — had not the most merciful Lord, moved by mercy, given His grace through Christ; so that the human race, relieved by receiving the forgiveness of sins, might come to its senses for the future, sin being beaten back and condemned. For, unburdened of evils and cleansed, he can resist the adversary, having recovered power against him, as long as he is helped by God. Here he brings in, as it were, a third law, the law of faith, more powerful, which he also calls grace: yet it has its origin from the spiritual law, because through it man has been set free; so that, since Moses gave a law and the Lord also gave one, they are called two, yet are understood as one, so far as meaning and providence are concerned. For the one is the beginner of salvation, the other its perfecter. But I do not mean that part of the law which concerns new moons and circumcision and foods, but that which belongs to the sacrament of God and to discipline. By the grace of God through Christ, then, man has been set free from the body of this death. He means the death which he showed above to have been devised through sin for the destruction of man in the underworld, which is called the second; and the body of death is all sins together: for many things are one body, each of them like a member, devised by one author. Rescued from these by the grace of God through baptism, man has escaped the death spoken of above. For he would not say, while placed in a fleshly body, that he had been rescued from that very body; but he called "body" that which above he shows is destroyed through baptism and the keeping of the Law. For when he says, *from the body of death*, he shows that there is another body which is not of death. *So then with the mind I myself serve the law of God.* When he says "the law of God", he means both the law of Moses and the law of Christ. *But with the flesh the law of sin. I myself*: that is, I who have been set free from the body of death. He is in the body: how has he been set free from the body of death, unless because he has been set free from all evils? For the forgiveness of sins takes away all sins. Set free, then, from the body of death by the grace of God through Christ, *with the mind* or soul *I serve the law of God, but with the flesh the law of sin* — that is, of the devil, who, through the flesh subject to him, thrusts evil suggestions upon the soul. Since man is double, then, made of flesh and of mind, he said that he serves the law of God with the mind: because the mind is devoted to God, and, having recovered its own power, can fight against sin, which works through the flesh. For since the flesh has been corrupted and made subject to death — the flesh which had been made such that, by its fellowship and union with the soul, it should not die — it has taken on desires, whose burden, as it were, it passes on to the mind, so that the mind too is weighed down. But what rejects it? The death of the grace of God. *With the mind I serve the law of God, but with the flesh the law of sin*, as has been said. For now the mind is free, and, called back into a good habit, with the Holy Spirit's help it can scorn evil suggestions: for authority has been given back to it, by which it may dare to resist the enemy. For he who is no longer liable cannot be tracked down against his will. But the flesh, since it has no judgment and is not capable of discerning — for it is a brute nature — cannot close the entrance against the enemy, so that he should not come in and urge on the mind things contrary to it: and so he says, *With the flesh I serve the law of sin.* For since one man consists of flesh and soul, with the part by which he is wise he serves God, and with the other, by which he is dull, the law of sin. But if man had remained as he was made, the enemy would have had no power to come to his flesh and whisper contrary things to his soul. But the sentence given against Adam (Genesis 3:17) stood in the way of the whole man's being restored by the grace of Christ to his original state: for it would have been unjust to undo a sentence lawfully pronounced. So, the sentence remaining, the providence of God found a remedy, that salvation, which man had lost by his own fault, might be given back to him: so that, healed, he might believe that his adversary, overcome by the power of Christ, would not dare — the sentence of the first death being cancelled — to claim man for himself, the race of Adam being united, lest, now abiding wholly immortal, he should be restored to the making of his first origin.
