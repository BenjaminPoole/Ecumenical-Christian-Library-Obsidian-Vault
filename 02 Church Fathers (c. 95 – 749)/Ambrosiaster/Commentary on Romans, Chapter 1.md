# Commentary on Romans, Chapter 1

*While almost all of the works in this vault use Victorian translations, due to the English translations of this commentary being in copyright, it was machine-translated (by Claude, 2026) from the public-domain Latin. It has not been checked by a human translator.*

*Commentary on Paul's Epistle to the Romans, long printed among the works of Ambrose. Its author, whom Erasmus first called "Ambrosiaster", wrote at Rome while Damasus was bishop there (366–384). Chapter 1 of the Epistle; the verse numbers are the Benedictine editors'.*

## Prologue

That a fuller knowledge of things may be had, their beginnings must first be sought out; for the reason of a matter can be set out more easily if its origin is learned. When we have shown the occasion and the reason of the letter's being sent, what we say may be seen to be true.

It is agreed, then, that in the apostles' times there were Jews living at Rome, because they lived under the Roman rule. Those of them who had believed handed on to the Romans that, while professing Christ, they should keep the Law. The Romans, having heard the fame of Christ's mighty works, were ready to believe, as being prudent men: and not without reason called prudent, since, though badly led in, they were at once set right, and remained in it. These believers in Christ from among the Jews, then, as we are given to understand, did not accept that God was from God, thinking it contrary to the one God; and therefore he says that they had not obtained the spiritual grace of God, and so lacked confirmation. These are they who had also subverted the Galatians, so that they fell away from the tradition of the apostles: and the Apostle is angry with the Galatians for this reason, that having been well taught, they had been easily drawn aside. But with the Romans he ought not to be angry; rather he should even praise their faith: because, without seeing any signs of mighty works, or any of the apostles, they had received the faith of Christ, though after the Jewish rite, in word rather than in understanding; for the mystery of Christ's cross had not been set out to them. That is why, when some arrived who had believed rightly, questions arose about eating meat and not eating it, and whether the hope that is in Christ was enough, or whether the Law too must be kept.

He writes to the Romans, then, in four ways, convicting the race of men from the beginning by the reason of nature; for these are the head of all the nations, so that in them all the rest of the Gentiles may learn. The first way is that in which he shows what he is, and what he was, and whose he is; and in this he strikes at the heresies as well. The second way is that in which he argues that by the reason of nature they did not submit themselves to the one God, and did shameful and foul things with one another, and were despised by God for it; so that believers may give thanks. The third way is that they spurned the Law that was given; and so he sets the Jews before the Greeks. The fourth way is that in which he teaches that the Jews swerved from the Law and from God's promise in the matter of Christ, and became like the Gentiles; so that both stand in need of God's mercy, hoping for salvation not through the Law but through faith in Christ Jesus.

This is why he works with all diligence to take them out from under the Law — because *the Law and the prophets were until John* (Luke 16:16) — and to set them in faith in Christ alone; and he defends the Gospel as though against the Law, not destroying the Law but preferring Christianity. For he maintains that Christ was promised in such a way that, once He had been preached, the Law should cease: not the whole of it, because what was made was a shortening of the Law, which should give salvation in brief. For many things had been handed to the men of old because of the hardness of their hearts, so as to be a burden; and these God's mercy has shortened through Christ, the forgiveness of what was past having been given. He is ungrateful to God's mercy, then, who wishes to be under the Law: for Moses had said, *Thus shall ye make the children of Israel fearful* (Leviticus 15:31), so that wherever they turned they should meet the Law, and should never be wholly without anxiety. So, to teach that the hope of life and salvation is to be placed in Christ without the Law, and that He is Lord of all, he begins thus:

## Romans 1

*(Verse 1.) Paul, a servant of Jesus Christ.* Among our forefathers names were given for a reason, as Isaac for laughter and Jacob for the heel; so he too was called Saul for his restlessness. But after he believed, he calls himself Paul instead of Saul, that is, changed. And since Saul, in the sense given above, is interpreted restlessness or temptation, when he came to the faith of Christ he calls himself Paul, that is, as one who from a tempter has become quiet and lowly, or little; because our faith is peace. For whereas before, out of the restlessness of Judaism, he brought temptations on the servants of God, afterwards he himself suffered temptations for the sake of the hope which before, for love of Judaism, he had denied. In professing himself a servant of Christ Jesus, he shows that he has put off the Law.

And so he set down both names, that is, Jesus Christ, to mark the person both of God and of man: because in both He is Lord, as the apostle Peter also testifies, saying, *He is Lord of all.* Since, then, He is both Lord and God — as David says, *Know ye that the Lord He is God* (Psalm 100:3) — which the heresies deny. For Marcion, out of hatred of the Law, sees fit to deny Christ and His body while professing Jesus; while the Jews and Photinus, out of zeal for the Law, deny that Jesus is God. For whenever he says either Jesus or Christ, he sometimes signifies God, sometimes man, as in this place among others: *And one Lord Jesus, by whom are all things* (1 Corinthians 8:6), which certainly belongs to the Son of God as He is God; and in another place: *And Jesus increased in age and wisdom* (Luke 2:52), which certainly befits a man.

*Called to be an apostle.* Because he knew God and confesses Him, and was made a fit servant, he shows that he has been promoted, saying, *Called to be an apostle*, that is, sent by the Lord to do His business. By this he shows that he who serves not the Law but Christ has merit with God; for *the Son of man is Lord even of the sabbath* (Matthew 12:8).

*Separated unto the Gospel of God.* The Gospel of God is God's good news, by which sinners are called together to pardon. The Apostle, since in Judaism he had held the place of a teacher, being a Pharisee, says that he has been separated from the preaching of Judaism unto the Gospel of God: so that, setting the Law aside, he might preach Christ, who would justify those who believe in Him, as the Law could not. Yet this is not against the Law, but for the Law: for the Law itself says that this would come, as Isaiah the prophet says, *There shall come out of Sion he that shall deliver and turn away captivity from Jacob; and this is my covenant unto them, when I shall take away their sins.* If, then, the law of God was given through Moses, and the preaching of the new Law is God's too, what difference is there, that he should say he has been moved from the Law which God gave to the Gospel of God? It is like one who goes up from the second step to the first, and from good to better. For the Law was given by God for the correction of conduct; but the Gospel of God is that by which the mystery of God is made known, which was hidden from the ages in God: which is Christ. All who are invited to it receive a double gift to take away with them: for they receive the forgiveness of sins, and they become sons of God, so that they can no longer die the second death. Hence the Lord says in the Gospel: *This is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom thou hast sent* (John 17:3).

*(Verse 2.) Which he had promised afore.* To prove that the hope of faith in Christ is sure and whole, he says that His Gospel had been promised by God long before; and to teach from this that He was a fit surety of life, he shows that witness was borne to Him before He came. Peter the apostle bears equal witness: *For there is none other name under heaven given, whereby we must be saved* (Acts 4:12).

*By his prophets.* To show more plainly that Christ's coming was a saving one, he pointed also to the persons through whom God sealed the promise, so that it might be seen from them how true and how great the promise is. For no one has a mean thing announced by great forerunners.

*In the holy Scriptures.* He added this to crown his true declaration, to give believers greater confidence, and to commend the Law. For the Scriptures are holy, which condemn vices as well; and in them is contained the mystery of the one God, and of the incarnation of the Son of God for the salvation of men, with signs and wonders for witnesses.

*(Verse 3.) Concerning his Son.* It was fitting that, since God was promising His own Son to the world, He should promise Him through illustrious men; so that from them it might be known how very mighty He was who was preached; so that He might place His coming beforehand in the holy Scriptures; and so that what had been preached in the holy Scriptures could not be thought false.

*Which was made of the seed of David according to the flesh.* Him who was Son of God according to the Holy Spirit, that is, according to God — for God is a spirit, and is without doubt holy — he says was made the Son of God according to the flesh, of the seed of David; as it is written, *And the Word was made flesh* (John 1:14): so that Christ Jesus is now the one Son of God and of man; so that, as He is true God, He should be true man as well. And He will not be true man unless He is of flesh and soul, so as to be complete. For He who was Son of God from eternity was unknown to the creation; but when He willed to be made manifest for the salvation of men, He had to become visible and bodily: because He willed both to be known by His mighty works, and to wash man clean of his sins in the flesh, death being conquered. And so He is made of the seed of David, that, as He was born King of God before the ages, so according to the flesh He might take a royal birth from a king, being made by the working of the Holy Spirit of a virgin — that is, born — so that, reverence being kept for Him, He should be known by this to be more than man: since He differed from birth by the law of men, as had been foretold by the prophet Isaiah, *Behold, a virgin shall conceive* (Isaiah 7:14); so that, when a new thing was seen to have been done, and a thing worthy of praise, a certain providence of God concerning the visiting of the human race should be discerned to be at hand.

*(Verse 4.) Who was predestinated the Son of God in power, according to the spirit of sanctification, by the resurrection of the dead, Jesus Christ our Lord.* In saying "Son of God" he signified God the Father; and in adding "the spirit of sanctification" he showed the mystery of the Trinity. This one, then, who, being made flesh, was hidden as to what He was, was predestinated, according to the spirit of sanctification, to be shown in power to be the Son of God, when He rose from the dead; as it is written in the eighty-fourth psalm, *Truth is sprung out of the earth* (Psalm 85:11). For all doubt and mistrust was trodden down and crushed by His resurrection: since, while He was still hanging on the cross, the centurion, seeing the great works, confesses Him Son of God (Matthew 27:54); and the disciples doubted at His death, as Cleophas says at Emmaus, *We trusted that it had been he which should have redeemed Israel* (Luke 24:21). And the Lord Himself says, *When ye have lifted up the Son of man, then shall ye know that I am he* (John 8:28); and again, *If I be lifted up from the earth, I will draw all things unto me* (John 12:32): that is, then I shall be known to be Lord of all. He did not say "by the resurrection of Jesus Christ", but "by the resurrection of the dead", because the resurrection of Christ bestowed a general resurrection. For this seems the greater power and victory in Christ: that He should work dead by the same power by which He had worked living. When this was done it appeared that He had made sport of death, to redeem us; which is why he calls Him our Lord.

*(Verse 5.) By whom we have received grace and apostleship, for obedience to the faith among all nations, for his name.* After the resurrection, the Son of God, made manifest in power, gave grace, justifying sinners, and named apostles, of whom he here calls himself a companion: so that the apostleship should go with the grace of God's gift, not as the Jews have their apostles. From God the Father, then, through Christ the Lord, they received this power: to make the Lord's teaching acceptable by signs, in the Lord's stead; so that the faithless Jews, seeing the multitudes marvel at that power in His servants which they had envied in the Saviour, should be the more tormented. For power is the witness of teaching: so that what was preached, being incredible to the world, should be made credible by deeds. He calls the apostles, then, sent men, sent to preach the faith to all nations, that they might obey and be saved; so that the gift of God might be seen to be granted no longer to the Jews alone, but to all nations as well; and that this is God's will, to have mercy on all in Christ and through Christ, His deputies preaching — which is the meaning of *for his name*; as he says elsewhere, *For whom we are ambassadors* (2 Corinthians 5:20).

*(Verse 6.) Among whom are ye also the called of Jesus Christ.* That is: we being ambassadors among all nations for the name of Jesus Christ, among whom you too are called. For the gift of God has been sent to all; so that when they hear that they are called among the rest, they should know that they ought not to live under the Law, since the other nations received the law of Christ without the law of Moses.

*(Verse 7.) To all that be in Rome, beloved of God, called to be saints.* Though he writes to the Romans, he shows that he is writing to those who are in the love of God. Who are these, but those who think rightly of the Son of God? These are holy, and are called "called". For those who live under the Law understand Christ amiss, and their thinking is an affront to God the Father, since they doubt whether the hope of salvation is complete in Christ. Therefore they are not holy, nor are they called "called".

*Grace to you and peace from God our Father, and the Lord Jesus Christ.* He says that grace and peace are with those who believe rightly. It is grace, because they have been loosed from their sins; and peace, because from enemies they have been reconciled to the Creator; as the Lord says, *Into whatsoever house ye enter, and they receive you, say, Peace be to this house* (Luke 10:5). And to teach that without Christ there is no peace and no hope, he added that grace and peace are not from God the Father only, but from Christ Jesus as well. He calls God our Father because of our origin, since all things are from Him; and Christ our Lord, because, redeemed by His blood, we have been made sons of God.

*(Verse 8.) First, I thank my God through Jesus Christ for you all, that your faith is spoken of throughout the whole world.* The preface ended, he declares his joy before all else, as the Apostle of the Gentiles: that the Romans, who ruled the world, had submitted themselves to the Christian faith, which to the prudent of this world seems lowly and foolish. Though there were many things for which he might rejoice in the Romans — for they were noted for their learning, and eager for good works, and more zealous to act well than to speak well, which is not far from divine religion — yet he says that he rejoices first in this: that their faith was running everywhere. Though it was not according to the rule of truth handed down by its authors, yet because they had begun, with Christ's name set between, to worship what was from the one God, he gives thanks, knowing that they could go forward. By this he shows his love towards them as well, when he rejoices with them in the good they have begun and urges them on to progress. He says that he gives thanks to his God only, because He was not yet fully theirs; since from Him is all fatherhood (Ephesians 3:15). But because the whole ordering of our salvation is from God indeed, but through Christ — not through the Law, nor through any prophet — he says that he gives thanks to God indeed, but through Christ, because the fame of their faith was the furtherance of many; so that he refers this very thing to God's providence through Christ. For either those who had believed rejoiced, being strengthened; and the rest, because they saw their rulers made believers; or at least those who had not believed could believe by their example. For the lesser easily does what he has seen done by the greater.

*(Verse 9.) For God is my witness, whom I serve with my spirit in the gospel of his Son, that without ceasing I make mention of you always in my prayers.* To win their love, he brings as witness God whom he serves and to whom he also prays for them: serving not in the Law but in the Gospel of His Son; that is, not in what Moses the servant handed down, but in what the most beloved Son taught. For as far as the servant is from the master, so far is the Gospel from the Law: not that the Law is bad, but that the Gospel is better. So he serves God in the Gospel of His Son, to show that it is God's will that men believe in Christ. *Whom I serve*: how? *In my spirit*, he says: not in circumcision made with hands, nor in new moons and the sabbath and the distinction of foods, but in the spirit, that is, in the mind. Because God is a spirit, He ought to be served with the spirit, or rather with the soul: for He who is served with the soul is served by faith. This the Lord too said to the Samaritan woman, who thought that God wished to be worshipped on the mountain: *The hour cometh, and now is, when the true worshippers shall worship the Father in spirit and in truth: for the Father seeketh such to worship him. God is a Spirit* (John 4:23–24). Those who worship Him, then, must worship in spirit and in truth; and it is not the place that commends the prayer, but the devout mind. That is: to worship in truth, in the spirit, God who is spirit, and Christ; the one from whom are all things, and the one through whom are all things (1 Corinthians 8:6). Such worshippers the Father seeks. He insists, then, that he is mindful of them in his prayers, to sow love in them; for he makes them long for him. For who would not love one whom he hears to be mindful of him? If they had gladly received teaching under the name of Christ, with the words corrupted, from men who had not been sent, how much more would they long to hear one whom they knew to be an apostle, and whose words were accompanied by power!

*(Verse 10.) Making request, if by any means now at length I might have a prosperous journey by the will of God to come unto you.* He shows the purport of his prayer for them: he says that he asks God that he may come to the City, for their furtherance, with the will of God, whose gift he preaches. For furtherance comes when what is to be done is done with the will of God. He prays, then, that for some cause or other an occasion might now be given him to come to the City, since he was busy preaching to others; counting the journey prosperous if he came by God's will, because the will of God is provident. A prosperous journey, then, is to undergo the toil of travel not in vain; and so he asks that God would bring it about by calling them to His grace. He speaks from the eagerness of his heart: for he longs for them, knowing that it will profit both them and himself; as he says elsewhere, *For what is our joy, or crown? Are not even ye in the coming of the Lord?* (1 Thessalonians 2:19). For the Apostle's fruit is the richer if he gains many. Moreover the joy is greater if the mighty of the world are turned to God: for the more formidable enemies are, the more needful they are once reconciled. And so the occasion was given to his wish in this way, that he was compelled to appeal to Caesar, and was sent for another reason to the city of Rome, God so willing, that he might fulfil the purpose of his wish. And so, when he was in the shipwreck, the Lord stood by him, saying, *Fear not, Paul: for as thou hast testified of me in Jerusalem, so must thou bear witness also at Rome* (Acts 27:24).

*(Verse 11.) For I long to see you, that I may impart unto you some spiritual gift, to the end ye may be established.* This establishing requires three persons: God who grants, the Apostle who imparts, and the people who receive. Here, then, he shows the intent of his longing, and what his wish was for them. For when he says, *that I may impart unto you a spiritual gift*, he shows that they had reached only a carnal understanding: because under the name of Christ they had come to hold not the things that Christ had taught, but the things that had been handed on by the Jews. And he desired to come the sooner, to draw them away from this tradition and hand on to them the spiritual gift, to gain them for God, making them sharers in spiritual grace, so that they might be perfect in their profession and their faith. From this we are given to understand that above he praised not their faith but their readiness and their goodwill towards Christ: for, while professing themselves Christians, they lived under the Law in simplicity, as it had been handed to them. For God's mercy was given for this, that the Law should cease, as I have often said already: because God, taking thought for human weakness, decreed to save the human race by faith alone, added to the natural law. Yet why, when he corrects them by letter and draws them away from a carnal understanding, does he say that his presence is needed to impart spiritual grace to them, though what he writes is spiritual? It is only because things said one way are commonly wrested to mean another, as the heretics do. So he desires to be present and hand on to them the teaching of the Gospel in the sense in which he writes it, lest under the authority of his letter their error should not be removed but confirmed: for when present he could persuade by power what he could not persuade by words, so that he might profit them the more by these additions.

*(Verse 12.) That is, that I may be comforted together with you by the mutual faith both of you and me.* He says he would be comforted with them if they laid hold of spiritual things: because, though he rejoices with them in their faith, he grieves that the faith they had received was not right. Such was the Apostle's feeling, that he grieved over the faults of others as if they were his own. And so, he says, let us be comforted by one and the same faith: so that there should be one comfort if they were made to be of one faith in Christ, and the imparting of spiritual grace, the Apostle preaching the Gospel, should bring this about.

*(Verse 13.) Now I would not have you ignorant, brethren, that oftentimes I purposed to come unto you, but was let hitherto.* He shows his purpose and his wish, which he does not doubt they knew of through those brethren who came to the City from Jerusalem or the neighbouring cities on account of their religion, such as Aquila and Priscilla, who made his wish known to the Romans. Since he often wished to come and was prevented, it came about that he wrote a letter, lest, held long in a bad practice, they should not easily be corrected. He calls them brethren not only because they had been born again, but because there were among them, though few, those who thought rightly; which is why he said, *called to be saints* (Romans 1:7). Yet what does *called to be saints* mean? If they are already saints, how are they called to be made holy? This belongs to God's foreknowledge: those whom God knows will be saints are already saints in His sight, and remain called. Yet he says he was prevented until the time the letter was sent — by God, of course, who, knowing them still unready, directed the Apostle to other cities which were already able to receive the truth. For though they lived under the name of the Saviour, they were held back by carnal vices, so that they were not yet worthy to learn spiritual things. So, when Paul and Silas wished at one time to go into Bithynia, they were forbidden by the Holy Spirit (Acts 16:7): why, unless He knew that the disposition was still lacking? Whereas at Corinth the Apostle is admonished, the Lord saying, *Speak, and hold not thy peace: for I have much people in this city* (Acts 18:9–10). So it is not idly that he says he was prevented; he set out the reasons for his wishes as well, and urged them to prepare themselves, so that, hearing that spiritual grace would be imparted to them, they might make themselves worthy to receive it by abstaining from carnal vices.

*That I might have some fruit among you also, even as among other Gentiles.* He declares that he desires to come to them for the common good: that they too may obtain the salvation of spiritual grace, holding their profession of faith confirmed; and that he may have with God the fruit of his ministry, by which he provokes them to the right faith by the example of the other nations. For one is readier for a thing handed to him if he sees many assent to it.

*(Verse 14.) I am debtor both to the Greeks and to the Barbarians; both to the wise and to the unwise.* He calls himself a debtor to those he names, because he was sent for this, to preach to all; and by this he shows that all are debtors as well. For to confess God the Creator, from whom are all things and through whom are all things, is both the honour that is owed and the salvation of him who confesses. By Greeks, then, he meant the Gentiles, but those who are called Romans whether by birth or by adoption; by Barbarians those who are not Romans, whose race is hostile, and who are Gentiles. And by the wise he meant those who, learned in worldly reasonings, are called wise in this world: whether they are watchers of the stars, or give themselves to measures, or numbers, or the art of grammar, rhetoric, or music. To all these he shows that these things profit nothing, and that they are not truly wise unless they believe in Christ. The foolish he called those who, following simplicity, are unskilled in these things. To all these he testifies that he is sent to preach. Of the Jews he said nothing, because he is the teacher of the Gentiles. And so he professes himself a debtor: because he received the teaching for this, to hand it on, and in handing it on to gain.

*(Verse 15.) So, as much as in me is, I am ready to preach the gospel to you that are at Rome also.* Though he says he was sent to preach to all nations, yet he declares himself ready to hand on the Gospel of God's grace to the Romans, among whom is the head and seat of the Roman rule. For it belongs to the good and the quiet of the members if the head is not unquiet. So he wishes for the peace of the Romans, so that Satan may not boast much, and he himself may have a richer fruit of his work.

*(Verse 16.) For I am not ashamed of the gospel: for it is the power of God unto salvation to every one that believeth.* In saying this he touches those from whom they had received a faith that was not right. The apostles' teaching, on the other hand, was commended by power: so that, because what was preached seemed incredible, the signs and wonders done by the apostles should be a witness that there was no reason to distrust what was said by men in whom there was such power — for no one doubts that words yield to power; and so, since no wonders had been seen from those others, their preaching was without the power of God. So he says that he is not ashamed of the Gospel of God, but that they are ashamed: because what they had handed on came under reproof, was confirmed by no witness at all, and was at odds with the apostolic teaching. It is the power of God, then, which invites to faith and gives salvation to every one who believes, forgiving sins and justifying; so that he who is sealed with the mystery of the cross cannot be held by the second death. For the preaching of Christ's cross is the token of death overcome, as the apostle John says: *For this purpose the Son of God was manifested, that he might destroy the works of the devil* (1 John 3:8): so that every believer is not held by death, because he has the sign that death has been conquered.

*To the Jew first, and also to the Greek.* That is, to him who is of the race of Abraham, and to him who is of the Gentiles: for by "Greek" he means a Gentile, and by "Jew" one who is of the race of Abraham. For they began to be called Jews from the time of Judas Maccabaeus, who, when the people were being destroyed, withstood the sacrileges of the Gentiles, and, trusting in God, gathered the people together and defended his race. He was of the sons of Aaron. So, though he puts the Jew first for the fathers' sake, yet he says that he too stands in need of the gift of the Gospel of Christ in the same way. If, then, even the Jew is not justified except through faith in Christ Jesus, what need is there to be under the Law?

*(Verse 17.) For therein is the righteousness of God revealed from faith to faith.* He says this because in him who believes, whether he is a Jew or a Greek, the righteousness of God is revealed. He calls it the righteousness of God because He freely justifies the ungodly through faith, without the works of the Law; as he says elsewhere, *That I may be found in him, not having mine own righteousness, which is of the Law, but that which is of faith* (Philippians 3:9). The righteousness which is from God in faith — this, he says, is revealed in the Gospel, when He gives man the faith by which he may be justified. For in him who believes and confesses, the truth and righteousness of God are shown: it is God's righteousness, because He gave what He promised. And so he who believes that he has obtained what God had promised through His prophets proves God righteous, and is a witness to His righteousness. *From faith to faith*: what does *from faith to faith* mean, but that there is God's faith in what He promised concerning Himself, and the faith of man, who believes Him who promises; so that from the faith of God who promises to the faith of man who believes, the righteousness of God is revealed? For in him who believes God appears righteous; but in him who does not believe He seems unrighteous: for he who does not believe that God has given what He promised denies that God is true. This he says against the Jews, who deny that this is the Christ whom God promised.

*As it is written, The just shall live by faith* (Habakkuk 2:4). For this reason he turns to the example of the prophet Habakkuk, to declare that it was shown long ago that the just man lives by faith and not by the Law: that is, that a man is justified before God not through the Law, but through faith. For the life that is by faith is not this present life, but the life to come; because the just man lives by faith, but with God.

*(Verse 18.) For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men, who hold the truth of God in unrighteousness.* As in him who believes the righteousness of God is revealed, as I said above, so in him who does not believe ungodliness and unrighteousness are revealed. For God is seen to be angry with them from the very fabric of the heavens: for He made the stars so beautiful for this reason, that from them it might be known how great and how wonderful their Creator is, and He alone be worshipped. Hence it is written in the psalm: *The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork* (Psalm 19:1). Through the natural law, then, he makes the human race guilty: for by the law of nature, with the fabric of the world bearing witness, they could have grasped that God the Maker alone is to be loved — which Moses put in writing (Deuteronomy 6:5). But they became ungodly by not worshipping the Creator; and unrighteousness appears in them, since, seeing, they turn a blind eye to the truth and do not confess the one God.

*(Verse 19.) Because that which may be known of God is manifest in them.* The knowledge of God is made manifest from the fabric of the world. For, so that God, who by nature is invisible, might be known even by visible things, He made a work which by its visibility should make its Maker manifest, so that the uncertain might be known through the certain; and so that He who made this work, which no other can, should be believed to be the God of all. *For God hath shewed it unto them*: that is, He made a work through which He might be known by faith.

*(Verse 20.) For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made.* It is the same sense, which he repeats, to teach more fully that, since the power and majesty of God cannot in themselves be beheld by the eyes of a creature, they are seen, being understood, through the work of the world. In this way, then, he makes guilty those who have lived without the law, both the law of nature and the law of Moses: for by the habit of sinning they buried the law of nature and forgot it; and the law which had been given for their reformation they would not accept; so that they became liable to condemnation in two ways. *Even his eternal power and Godhead; so that they are without excuse.* That ungodliness might not be excused at all, he added that the power of God and His eternal Godhead were known to men as well, and that they had grown dull, as with a kind of stupor, to honouring God, though they knew both that He is and that He works for their good: for no one doubts that He decreed the things that are brought forth for the uses of men as the year comes round. His eternal power, then, is Christ, through whom He established what was not, and it abides in Him. Though His person was not known in former times, yet His works are manifest. And His Godhead, because the elements of things continue in the work appointed them. *So that they are without excuse*: for one who is convicted of guilt in many ways cannot be excused.

*(Verse 21.) Because that, when they knew God, they glorified him not as God, neither were thankful.* So far were they from not knowing Him that they confessed that there is one beginning from which all things took their start, things in heaven and on earth and under the earth; and that this One assigned to all things their properties and offices by nature; and, knowing this, they did not give thanks. He speaks of those of former times, to correct those of the present and the future. *But became vain in their imaginations.* They became vain: for, seeing the world fitted out with wonderful adornment by Him whom they had known to be its Maker, they turned a blind eye. *And their foolish heart was darkened.* A cloud of error covered their heart: for when they ought to have honoured the Creator the more for the beautiful things He made, they grew dull, and, leaving Him, said that the things that are seen were enough for their worship by themselves.

*(Verse 22.) Professing themselves to be wise, they became fools.* For they think themselves wise because they suppose they have searched out the reasons of nature, examining the courses of the stars and the qualities of the elements, while despising the Lord of them; and so they are fools, since, if these things are to be praised, how much more their Creator! Yet, when shamed, they are accustomed to use a wretched excuse for neglecting God, saying that through these one may go to God, as one comes to the king through his officers. Come now: is anyone so mad, or so forgetful of his own safety, as to claim the king's honour for an officer — when, if any were found even to be discussing such a thing, they would rightly be condemned as guilty of treason? And these men do not think themselves guilty, who give the honour of God's name to a creature, and, leaving the Lord, worship their fellow-servants: as though there were anything more left to be kept for God! For the reason one goes to the king through tribunes or officers is that the king is, after all, a man, and does not know to whom he should entrust the commonwealth. But to win the favour of God, from whom nothing is hidden — for He knows the deserts of all — there is no need of a patron, but of a devout mind. For wherever such a one speaks to Him, He will answer him.

*(Verse 23.) And changed the glory of the uncorruptible God into an image made like to corruptible man.* So blinded was their heart that they changed the majesty of the invisible God, whom they had come to know from these works, not into men but — what is worse, and a crime without excuse — into the likeness of men: so that the form of corruptible man, that is, the image of a man, is called God by them; so that, while they dare not honour the living with this name, they receive the images of the dead into the glory of God. What dullness, what folly, to call these men wise, to their own condemnation, with whom an image counts for more than the truth, and the dead for more than the living! For, departing from the living God, they favour the dead, among whom they themselves are numbered; as it is written in the Wisdom of Solomon, *Being mortal, he worketh a dead thing with wicked hands* (Wisdom 15:17).

*And to birds, and fourfooted beasts, and creeping things.* By adding these he heaped up the penalty of their dullness, so that such men are beyond folly and vanity. For they so lessened the majesty and glory of God as to give the honour of God's likeness to the least and smallest of these. For the Babylonians first called God the image of one Bel, once a dead man, who is said to have held rule over them. They worshipped also the serpent, the dragon, which Daniel the man of God slew (Bel and the Dragon 26), whose likeness they keep. The Egyptians worshipped a four-footed beast, which they called Apis, in the likeness of a cow — an evil which Jeroboam imitated (1 Kings 12:28), setting up calves in Samaria to which the Jews should sacrifice; and birds, because the pagans held the rites of the raven. Of all these things that I have named the Egyptians worshipped images, and other things which there is no need to speak of now. These things were done by those who thought themselves wise in the world: for because, knowing the invisible God, they did not honour Him, by doing these things they could not be wise even in things that are visible. For one who is prudent in greater things is hardly unwise in the lesser.

*(Verse 24.) Wherefore God also gave them up to uncleanness through the lusts of their own hearts, to dishonour their own bodies between themselves.* Because, he says, they deified the images and likenesses of things, to the affront of God the Creator, they were given up to be mocked: given up, not to do what they did not wish, but to accomplish what they desired. And here too is God's goodness: for though it would have been just for them to be made subject so as to do what they did not wish, and be tormented by it — since even a good thing, if done against the will, is bitter and evil — yet these, turning away from God, were handed over to the devil. To hand over is to permit, not to incite or to send in; so that, helped by the devil, they should carry out in deed what they had conceived in their desires: for men of this kind could not hold a good thought. They were given up, then, *to uncleanness, to dishonour their own bodies between themselves*. While he recalls what is past, he points to the evils of the present time as well: for men are given up to this day to dishonour their own bodies. For there are men of that stock to this day who are said to dishonour their bodies with one another. When the thought of the soul is in the crime, the bodies are said to be dishonoured: why, unless because a stain on the body is the token of sin in the soul? For when the body is defiled, no one doubts that there is sin in the soul.

*(Verse 25.) Who changed the truth of God into a lie, and worshipped and served the creature more than the Creator, who is blessed for ever. Amen.* He says that they changed the truth of God into a lie: that is, they gave the name of God, who is true, to those who are not gods. For, taking from stones and wood and the other metals what they are, they give them what they are not; and this is to change the true into the false. For they are no longer called stone or wood, but God; and this is to serve the creature rather than the Creator. For they do not deny God, but they serve the creature: and by this they are not excused, but the more accused, because, knowing God, they do not honour Him. *Who is blessed for ever. Amen*: that is, truly. He says that God is *blessed for ever*, because God endures; whereas the ungodliness of a time gives honour to the gods of the Gentiles, and so it is not true. But in God truth abides. This blessing he assigns in another place to the Son of God, saying among other things: *And of whom as concerning the flesh Christ came, who is over all, God blessed for ever. Amen* (Romans 9:5). Either both belong to Christ, or he has said the same things of the Son as of the Father.

*(Verse 26.) For this cause God gave them up unto vile affections: for even their women did change the natural use into that which is against nature.* He testifies that these things came upon the human race from God's anger because of idolatry: that woman should desire woman for use with a shameful lust. Some interpret this otherwise, not seeing the force of what is said. For what is it to change the natural use into that which is against nature, but, setting aside the use that is granted, to use otherwise: so that one and the same part of the body of each sex offers itself for use otherwise than is granted? For if it is the part of the body which they suppose, how did they change the use of nature, when they have no such use given them by nature? He had already said above that they were given up to uncleanness, but had not shown the kind of that unclean work; which he now makes plain.

*(Verse 27.) And likewise also the men, leaving the natural use of the woman, burned in their lust one toward another; men with men working that which is unseemly.* Now he has made plain how what he said above of the women ought to be understood. For when he added, *Likewise also the men … burned in their lust*, he plainly shows the sin of the women. He did not say of the men that they changed the natural use among themselves, because no such use is granted to that part, but he said it of the women. And what wonder, when even today such women are found; so that, as that was invented by men, this too was invented by women? Women accuse one another of it, and men likewise. It is plain, then, that those who had already changed the truth of God into a lie should change the natural use as well into that use by which they would be dishonoured, being made guilty of the second death. For since Satan cannot give another law, having nothing of his own, he turns things granted and lawful into another order, so that, while men use them otherwise than they are granted, they become sin.

*And receiving in themselves that recompence of their error which was meet.* This, he says, is the recompence of despising God: shamefulness and defilement. For this is the first cause of this sin. For what is worse, what lower, than this sin? As idolatry is a most impious and most grievous offence, so its recompence is dreadful, and a most foul passion.

*(Verse 28.) And even as they did not like to retain God in their knowledge, God gave them over to a reprobate mind, to do those things which are not convenient.* For the errors of idols they were given over to do shameful things with one another, as has already been said. And because they thought these things went unpunished, judging God careless, and so to be neglected, this was added: that, dulled more and more, they should become readier to commit every evil; so that they believed God would not punish deeds which they did not doubt displeased men. Now he lists all the evils that were added to them besides, so that, turned back at least by this to natural understanding, they might perceive that these things had come upon them from God's anger.

*(Verses 29–30.) Being filled, he says, with all unrighteousness.* Here he has spoken in sum, and then he adds the members of unrighteousness: *Wickedness, uncleanness*, he says, *covetousness, maliciousness; full of envy, murder, debate, deceit, malignity; whisperers, backbiters, haters of God, despiteful, proud, boasters, inventors of evil things.* He named wickedness as the head of unrighteousness, and set down fornication and covetousness as its work. This fornication includes adultery too: for if he had said "adultery", he would have seemed to excuse fornication, as the Roman laws do; so he set down the lesser, that the greater might not be thought to go unpunished. Then he added *maliciousness*, whose disposition breeds envy and murders, strife and deceit. After these he added *malignity*, which begets whisperings and backbitings; and since these cannot but displease God, he says, *haters of God*; and because they do not please men either, *despiteful*, he says, *proud, boasters, inventors* — that is, authors — *of evil things*. Evil, then, did not always exist. But these men, having become imitators of their father the devil, invented the evil of idolatry, through which all vices were born into the world, and the greatest ruin. For the devil, whom Scripture testifies sins from the beginning (1 John 3:8), though he gives himself to a tyrant's presumption, yet has not dared to profess it so far as to say, "I am God." He said to the Lord among other things, *All this is delivered unto me* (Luke 4:6): he does not say, "It is from me," or "It is mine." And in the book of Job he asks that power be given him (Job 1:12). These men, therefore, are worse, who ascribe deity not only to the elements but to images as well.

*(Verse 31.) Disobedient to parents.* With how much insolence they were seized, that they did not acknowledge even their parents, whom they have as the authors of their birth, and who rejoiced that they were born; yet they despised those through whom they had begun to be! This is to be *without understanding, without love* — the love of God, that is; for the love of the flesh was in them. *Who, knowing the righteousness of God* by the law of nature (for they knew that these things displease God, but would not lay it to heart: *that they which commit such things are worthy of death*), so far knew God's righteousness that they do not deny that all these things they do are worthy of punishment and death. *Not only do the same, but have pleasure in them that do them*: for consent is a sharing.

*(Verse 32.) Who, knowing the judgment of God, understood not that they which commit such things are worthy of death: not only do the same, but have pleasure in them that do them.* All these evils are the body of sins, to which those who are enslaved are made subject to the same power, so that they do things that deserve punishment. For this cause of evils began with the Sodomites, with offence against God, as was said above, and has spread its branches into almost every part of the world, proclaiming idolatry as the cause of God's wrath: which is the first part of error and ungodliness, and which he condemns first; so that when this has been amended, the vices of conduct also may easily be corrected. For when the seed of wickedness is taken away, the fruits of maliciousness will wither; for a tree whose roots have been cut out bears no fruit. That is why Moses recorded the deeds of Sodom and Gomorrah (Genesis 19:24 ff.) and did not pass over their destruction in silence: to put a fear of this thing, that it might be shunned. This vice, then, and the defilement of a dishonoured life, is not committed by one who has God in his mind.

There are some who do not think themselves guilty if they do not themselves do what is evil, though they assent to those who do it. To assent is to be silent when one could rebuke; or, on hearing such tales, to flatter. For when the impure and malicious man knows that what he does is not unknown, and that he is not shunned at all, but is even held in honour, he perhaps glories in being such a man: nor can he be put to shame in these things when he sees himself favoured and humoured by those who are not such. And so it is that they furnish fuel for these men's sins, and therefore it is just that they should be held guilty of the same crime. There are others again who not only do evil, but also consent to those who do it: so that they not only do it, but consent to such men. Their maliciousness, then, is double. For those who do evil, and yet speak against others who do it, are not so bad; for knowing the wickedness, they do not punish it. But these are utterly wicked, who both do it and consent to those who do it, so that they do not even fear God; but, wishing evils to increase, they do not punish them at all, in order to persuade men that they are not to be shunned.
