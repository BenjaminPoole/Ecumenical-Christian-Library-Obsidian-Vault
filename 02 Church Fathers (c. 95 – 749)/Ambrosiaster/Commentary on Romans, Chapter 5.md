# Commentary on Romans, Chapter 5

*While almost all of the works in this vault use Victorian translations, due to the English translations of this commentary being in copyright, it was machine-translated (by Claude, 2026) from the public-domain Latin. It has not been checked by a human translator.*

*Commentary on Paul's Epistle to the Romans, long printed among the works of Ambrose. Its author, whom Erasmus first called "Ambrosiaster", wrote at Rome while Damasus was bishop there (366–384). Chapter 5 of the Epistle; the verse numbers are the Benedictine editors'.*

*(Verse 1.) Therefore being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ.* It is faith that makes us have peace with God, not the law: for faith reconciles us to God, taking away the sins which had made us God's enemies. And because the Lord Jesus is the minister of this grace, it is through Him that we have been brought to peace with God. For faith is greater than the law: for the law is for our sake, but faith is for God's; and the law belongs to discipline for the present, but faith to everlasting salvation. But he who does not think of Christ as he ought will not be able to come to the rewards of faith, because he does not hold the truth of the faith.

*(Verse 2.) By whom also we have access by faith into this grace wherein we stand, and rejoice in hope of the glory of the sons of God.* It is plain that through Christ we have access to the grace of God: for He is the mediator between God and men, who, raising us up by His teaching, has made us hope for the gift of God's grace, standing in faith in Him. "Standing", because before we lay fallen; but believing, we have been raised up, glorying in the hope of the splendour which God has promised us.

*(Verse 3.) And not only so, but we glory in tribulations also.* Since *we must through much tribulation enter into the kingdom of God* (Acts 14:22), he teaches that we should glory in these as well. Tribulation added to hope increases the reward: for tribulation is the token of a fixed hope, and bears witness towards the crown. Hence the Lord says: *Blessed are ye, when men shall persecute you, and shall say all manner of evil against you, for righteousness' sake. Rejoice, and be exceeding glad: for great is your reward in heaven* (Matthew 5:11–12). For to despise the things of the present, and the things that delight, and not to give way under pressure, in the hope of what is to come, is of great merit with God. And so we must glory in tribulations: because a man believes he will receive the more, the stronger he sees himself in tribulation. *Knowing that tribulation worketh patience.* Tribulation works patience in this way: if the tribulation itself suffers no stirring of weakness or doubt.

*(Verse 4.) And patience, experience.* It is plain that patience, if it is such as we have said, will be seen to be a firm proof. *And experience, hope.* Hope is not unfittingly spoken of in one who is seen to be proved: for he will be known to be worthy, as one who will receive a reward in the kingdom of God.

*(Verse 5.) And hope maketh not ashamed; because the love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost which is given unto us.* Hope does not put us to shame, though we are judged foolish and dull by the faithless for believing things that lack worldly reason: for we have in us, as a pledge of God's love, the Holy Spirit who has been given us. For the Holy Spirit, given to the apostles and to us, proves that God's promise is faithful: He granted that unlearned men should speak in different tongues, with interpretations, to confirm our hope, and also to commend God's love in us (Acts 2:4); so that, since it is impossible for those who are dear to Him to be deceived, He might make us secure about the promise; for it is God who promised, and He promised to those whom He wishes to hold dear. For since human words cannot establish the reason of our faith except by the witness of mighty works, which cry aloud though they are silent, our faith is proved reasonable, to the confusion of the prudent of this world, who with boldness of words fight with earthly arms against heavenly things, and with carnal against spiritual, and are not ashamed to call themselves prudent. For as the origin of a stranger cannot be proved in a foreign land, so the truth of our faith is a stranger on earth; and because its nature cannot be set out in words, it is commended by the witness of power, which is the greater thing.

*(Verses 6–7.) For when we were yet without strength, in due time Christ died for the ungodly. For scarcely for a righteous man will one die: yet peradventure for a good man some would even dare to die.* If Christ gave Himself to death for a time for unbelievers and enemies of God — for a time, since He rose again on the third day — how much more will He defend us with His help, now that we believe in Him? For He died for us for this purpose, to gain for us both life and glory. So if He died for enemies, we ought to understand how much He bestows on friends. Among men, then — that is, "according to time" — He was seen to die; for in the underworld He was judging the irreverent and wicked spirits, for the salvation of souls. There are times in the world, where the sun rises and sets, and the moon waxes and wanes, and day and night do not stay as they are: for a thing that is subject to time and age is always changeable. To this time, then, Christ died, when He went out of the body; but where there is no time or age, there He was found not only alive but victor. From this he wished to commend the Saviour's love towards us, when he says, *Scarcely for a righteous man will one die*: but Christ died for the ungodly. For if one will scarcely die for a righteous man, how can it come about that one should die for an ungodly man? And if perhaps one might dare to die for one good man — or perhaps would not dare, since he shows that both are hard — how can it be seen that anyone should dare to die for the ungodliness of many? For if one might perhaps dare to die for a righteous or a good man, drawn by a kind of pity, or by love of his good works, yet for the ungodly not only is there nothing to persuade one to die, but nothing even to move one to tears. But Christ died for the ungodliness of the people, and of a people not yet His own: to make credible all the things the world disbelieves. He made two steps, then, the righteous and the good: for though the righteous man too must be called good, yet he set down the kinds themselves, meaning the righteous by exercise and the good by nature, the one who is called innocent from simplicity. Hence, though the righteous man is of better merit than the good, so far as this place is concerned, yet he says that one might perhaps dare to die for a good man: meaning that because the case of innocence is the more pitiable, someone might perhaps be driven to this. Good parents wish to die for good sons — not to speak of wives for good husbands. For if we wish to set out the good and the righteous, we find that sometimes the righteous man is better, and sometimes the good is to be preferred. If he who is righteous by the law of God is better than the good, it is better than the good man who has not yet laboured with himself to enlarge himself with good things: for by exercise the righteous man will improve the good of his nature. But if he is righteous by the world's standard, the good man is preferred to him for his innocence, because such righteousness is not without severity. So every nature is good, while righteousness is the fruit of God's law that suits nature: and therefore righteousness is goodness, which is why the righteous are always called good as well. But the good are not always righteous, because they are called good not for their works but for their innocence; for righteousness is perfect goodness, when it fulfils by works the good things of its nature.

*(Verse 8.) But God commendeth his love toward us.* He commends His love in this way, that while men are still His enemies He is kind to them, and sends one to save them, though they certainly do not deserve it.

*(Verse 9.) In that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us. Much more then, being now justified by his blood, we shall be saved from wrath through him.* He says this because, if God allowed His Son to be killed for sinners, what will He do for the justified, but keep them from wrath — that is, keep them safe from the devil's mockery; so that they may be secure in the day of judgment, when vengeance begins to lay waste the faithless? For since God's goodness wills that none should perish, He gave a most worthy death: that to those who understand God's grace towards them He might add honour and glory, and on the ungrateful heap up punishment. The ungrateful are those who, when God calls, refuse, rejecting the grace of God, so as to remain in their purpose of error and malice.

*(Verse 10.) For if, when we were enemies, we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son, much more, being reconciled, we shall be saved by his life.* It is plain that, if God gave His Son up to death in order to reconcile us to Himself, how much more will He save us, once reconciled, by His life? For He who does good to enemies cannot fail to love friends. If the Saviour's death profited us while we were still ungodly, how much more will His life profit us once justified, when He rises from the dead? For as His death rescued us from the devil, so His life will set us free from the day of God's judgment.

*(Verse 11.) And not only so, but we also joy in God through our Lord Jesus Christ, by whom we have now received the atonement.* He teaches that we must not only give thanks to God for the salvation and security we have received, but also glory in God through Christ Jesus, who has deigned that we, from being ungodly and enemies, should be called His friends; so that we may rejoice at having obtained all benefits through Christ; so that, since we have known God through Him, glorying in Him, we may render Him honour equal to God the Father, as He Himself, a fit witness for Himself, proclaims: *That all men should honour the Son*, He says, *even as they honour the Father* (John 5:23). After the providence of God the Father, then, and the gift which He gave through Christ, to make the person of the Son dear to us — since we have been redeemed through the one Christ by the one God the Father — he added:

*(Verse 12.) Wherefore, as by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, in whom all have sinned.* Since above he has shown the grace of God given through Christ according to the order of truth, he now sets out that very order of the one God the Father through the one Christ His Son: so that, as Adam — one, that is, Eve, for she too is Adam — sinned in all, so the one Christ, the Son of God, conquered sin in all. And because he is showing the purpose of God's grace towards the human race, he began from Adam, who first sinned, to show the very beginnings of sin; to teach that the providence of the one God has, through one, set right what through one had fallen and been dragged down to death. This, then, is the one through whom we have been saved; and to Him, by His own will, we owe this reverence which we owe to God the Father. For the same Apostle says in another place, *He that in these things serveth Christ is acceptable to God* (Romans 14:18), though it is written, *Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve* (Deuteronomy 6:13). If, then, he says that God alone is to be served, and has commanded that Christ be served, Christ is in the unity of God, and is not unequal, nor another God: for when the law says that God alone is to be served, it is proclaimed that one who serves Christ jointly pleases God. *As by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin*, then, so through one Christ came the condemnation and death of sin, bestowing eternal life, as he sets out further on. *In whom* — that is, in Adam — *all have sinned.* He said *in whom* though he is speaking of the woman, because he referred not to the individual but to the kind. It is plain, then, that all have sinned in Adam as in a lump: for he, corrupted by sin, and all whom he begot, were born under sin. From him, then, all are sinners, because we are all from him. For he lost the gift of God when he transgressed, being made unworthy to eat of the tree of life, so that he died. Death is the dissolution of the body, when the soul is separated from the body. There is also another death, called the second, in hell, which we do not suffer by Adam's sin, but which is gained, on the occasion of it, by our own sins. From this the good are free; only that they were in the underworld — but in the upper part, as it were in a place of freedom — and could not go up to heaven: for they were held by the sentence given in Adam, the handwriting which was in the decrees, and which was blotted out by Christ's death (Colossians 2:14). And the sentence of the decree was that the body of the one man should be dissolved on earth, and that the soul, held in the bonds of the underworld, should suffer exile.

*(Verse 13.) For until the law sin was in the world: but sin is not imputed when there is no law.* He says that all sinned in Adam, as I mentioned above, and that until the law was given sin was not imputed: for men thought they sinned with impunity before God, though not before men. For the natural law had not altogether grown numb, since they knew that they ought not to do to others what they would not suffer themselves. Indeed, sin was so far from being unknown among men that Jacob, when Laban his father-in-law was looking for his idols in his camp, judged worthy of death the one with whom the theft should be found (Genesis 31:30 ff.); and Joseph, though by a false charge, was yet shut in prison as guilty (Genesis 39:20); and Pharaoh's baker and butler likewise suffered for an offence (Genesis 40:3); and Moses, having killed the Egyptian, was terrified by the law (Exodus 2:12). How, then, was sin not imputed when there was no law? Or how was it punished, unless the law was known? The natural law always exists, and was never unknown; but it was thought to have authority only for a time, and not to make men guilty before God as well. For it was not known that God would judge the human race; and so sin was not imputed, as if sin were not known before God — men declaring God careless. But when the law was given through Moses, it was made plain that God cares for human affairs, and that it will not go unpunished for those who do evil, though for some reason or other they escape at present. For if among themselves, with justice or nature as their teacher, they judged that sins should not go unpunished, how much more ought they to have known that God, whom they knew to be the Maker of the world, would require these things? Seeing that Sodom and Gomorrah were sentenced to perish by fire (Genesis 19:24), which forgetfulness had covered over; but Moses, to confirm that God would be judge, made it plain by writing it. But when, passing God by, they began to receive images to the honour of God, depraved in mind, they trampled on the part of the natural law which is the first. For the natural law has three parts. The first is this: that the Creator, being known, should be honoured, and that His splendour and majesty should not be assigned to any creature. The second part is moral: that is, that men should live well, with modesty governing them; for it befits a man who has knowledge of the Creator to rein in his life by law, lest that knowledge be in vain. The third part is teachable: that the knowledge of God the Creator, and the example of conduct, should be handed on to others, that they may learn how merit is laid up with the Creator. This is true and Christian prudence.

*(Verse 14.) Nevertheless death reigned from Adam to Moses.* Since sin was not imputed before the law was given through Moses, as I said, death reigned by the very impunity of its usurpation, knowing that men were given over to it. Death, then, reigned in the security of its dominion, both over those who escaped for a time and over those who were punished even here for their evil works. For it saw that all were its own, because *whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin* (John 8:34). For, thinking it went unpunished, they offended the more; readiest, though, about those sins which the world fostered as though they were lawful. And at this Satan rejoiced, secure that, because of Adam, he held in his possession man forsaken by God. *Death reigned*, then, *over them that had sinned after the similitude of Adam's transgression, who is the figure of him that was to come* — which we shall show in what follows. So it is plain that death did not reign over all, because not all sinned after the likeness of Adam's transgression: that is, not all sinned in contempt of God. And who are they who sinned in contempt of God, but those who, neglecting the Creator, served the creature, setting up for themselves gods to worship, to the affront of God? That is why the devil rejoiced in them, because he saw that they had become his imitators. For Terah, Abraham's father, and Nahor, and Laban, claimed gods of their own. And Adam's sin is not far from idolatry: for he transgressed thinking that, being a man, he would become God. For he judged that what the devil urged would profit him more than what God commanded, setting the devil in the place of God; and so he was made subject to the devil. Likewise these, passing God by, while they serve the creature, sin in a like way, not in the same way: for a likeness usually has something unlike in it. Nor can it be said that those too who sinned were like Adam, who received the command not to eat of the tree (Genesis 2:16): they sinned, not transgressing against God, but within the natural law. For he who understood — whether from tradition or by natural judgment — and worshipped God, sharing the honour of His name and majesty with no one: if he sinned, since it is impossible not to sin, he sinned under God, not against God, whom he perceived to be judge; and so death did not reign over such a man. But it reigned, as I said, over those who served the devil under the guise of idols. Because the law had not been published with authority, they could not foresee that God was judge. For the greatest part of the world did not know that God would be judge; and those over whom death did not reign were very few. Those over whom it reigned were, after this death which is called the first, taken up by the second, to the punishment and perdition to come. But those over whom it did not reign, because they did not sin after the likeness of Adam's transgression, were kept in hope for the Saviour's coming, in a place of freedom: as we read of Abraham, that, though he was in the underworld, he was yet set apart at a great distance, so that there was a great gulf between the righteous and the sinners (Luke 16:26). If there was a great gulf between the righteous and sinners, how much more between them and the ungodly: so that for the righteous there should be refreshment, for sinners heat, and for the ungodly burning; so that what each deserved should not be hidden before the judgment. And so death reigned over them, because it saw that the works of its own cunning had profited them to their punishment, as enemies. For it did not escape the devil that man was made in the world for this, to proclaim the rule of the one God, against which Satan transgressed.

As for the claim that in the Greek it is not so guarded — for they say it is written there that death reigned over those also who did not sin after the likeness of Adam's transgression — he seems to have embraced the whole here: so that, since death, that is, dissolution, came about through the envy of the devil, he says that death reigned even over those who did not sin; for they die, which is what Satan wishes. But if that were true, it should not have been said, *Death reigned from Adam to Moses*, if it reigns over all from the beginning to the end. Or perhaps he made steps: that it reigned from Adam to Moses, and from Moses to Christ, and from Christ to the end. To what purpose, then, would this way of speaking serve, when he is not shown to have spoken so? For he said, *Death reigned from Adam to Moses*, because the Law had not been made known — so that, once the Law was given, they should live under its authority, knowing what to beware of, so that death should not reign over them. He, then, who before the Law kept, with nature for his guide, what the Law afterwards commanded — can it be said that death reigned over him? For see what is written about it: *Sin*, he says, *is not imputed when there is no law*; and he added, *Nevertheless death reigned from Adam to Moses*, that is, at the time when the Law was not; and it agrees that, since death reigned before the Law was given, it should be said to have reigned over those who sinned after the likeness of Adam's transgression, as we mentioned above. For as after the Law was given death reigned over those who served idols or fornication, despising the Lawgiver, so too before the Law it certainly did not reign over those who perceived beforehand the meaning of the Law, honouring its Author. For death is said to have reigned because the knowledge of the one God had vanished from the earth. And so the Law, when it was given, began thus: *I am*, He says, *the Lord thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage. Thou shalt have no other gods before me. Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image, or any likeness* (Exodus 20:2–4). The Law was given, then, so that death should not reign; so that, the former sins being abolished, the human race should obey the one God. Hence the same Apostle says in another place, *Let not sin therefore reign in your mortal body, that ye should obey it* (Romans 6:12). By this saying he shows that death reigns even now, if the Law is despised. For what is the reign of death, but when its will is done, as it commands, to the ruin of him who obeys — the will of whose rule is the head of idolatry?

And yet the Greek manuscripts are urged against us in this way, as though they did not differ among themselves — which is the work of a love of contention. For when a man cannot win by his own authority, he corrupts the words of the law, so as to assert his own meaning as though it were the words of the law; so that it seems to be authority, not reason, that lays down the rule. But it is well known that some Latin manuscripts were translated long ago from old Greek manuscripts, which the simplicity of those times kept uncorrupted, and which it approves. But after men's minds fell away from concord, and they began to be tormented with questions by the heretics who stirred things up, many things were changed to suit human thinking, so that what seemed good to a man was contained in the text; which is why the Greeks themselves have differing manuscripts. I judge this to be the true reading, since reason, history and authority are all kept: for what is found fault with in the Latin manuscripts today is found set down so by the ancients, Tertullian, Victorinus and Cyprian.

The reign of death, then, began to be destroyed first in Judaea, because *in Judah is God known* (Psalm 76:1); but now it is being destroyed daily among all nations, as for the most part sons of the devil become sons of God. So death did not reign over all, but over those who sinned after the likeness of Adam's transgression, as I mentioned above. And Adam is the figure of Him that was to come for this reason: that even then God decreed in a mystery to set right through one, Christ, what had been sinned through one, Adam; as it is said in the Apocalypse of the apostle John: *The Lamb*, he says, *slain from the foundation of the world* (Revelation 13:8). And so he added:

*(Verse 15.) But not as the offence, so also is the free gift.* Because he said that the one Adam is the figure of the one Christ, he says, *But not as the offence, so also is the free gift*, lest he should be thought to mean that Adam's case was such as Christ's: for Adam is the figure of Christ only in this, that what one sinned, one set right. *For if through the offence of one many be dead, much more the grace of God, and the gift by grace, which is by one man, Jesus Christ, hath abounded unto many*: that is, if through the offence of one many have died, imitating his transgression, much more has the grace and gift of God abounded unto many who take refuge in it. For more obtain grace than have died by Adam's offence. From which it is plain that he did not mean this death which is common to all: for absolutely all die, and yet not all obtain grace. Nor is it meant that death reigned over all, but over those who died by Adam's offence, whom he says sinned after the likeness of Adam's transgression. These are they of whom he says that through the offence of one many have died, but that the grace of God abounds unto more: for the grace of God abounded both in those who are said to have died by Adam's offence, sinning in the same way, and in those who did not sin after this likeness of Adam's transgression. And those who, for their father's sin, by God's sentence were in the underworld — on them the grace of God abounded at the Saviour's descent, giving pardon to all, and they were carried up in triumph into heaven.

*(Verse 16.) And not as it was by one that sinned, so is the gift: for the judgment was by one to condemnation, but the free gift is of many offences unto justification.* It is plainly different: for by the one sin of Adam those were condemned who sinned after the likeness of his transgression; but the grace of God through Christ justified men not from one offence but from many, giving them forgiveness of sins. He says this to the glory of the goodness of God and of Christ: that though many are held by the second death in the lower part of the underworld because of Adam's offence, yet the gift of God's grace, when it would have been just for them to be punished, not only pardons them but justifies them.

*(Verse 17.) For if by one man's offence death reigned by one; much more they which receive abundance of grace and of the gift of righteousness shall reign in life by one, Jesus Christ.* We must observe that the sense is one and differs in nothing. For he says that death *reigned*, not that it reigns: because those who understood God's judgment through the Law have been taken out of its dominion. And it reigned because, without the Law being made known, there was no fear of God on earth. The sense above, then, is this: since death reigned from Adam to Moses over those who sinned after the likeness of Adam's transgression, much more will grace reign, by the abundance of God's gift, unto life, through the one Jesus Christ. For if death reigned, why should not grace reign the more, which justifies far more than those over whom death reigned? How much more must grace be believed to reign, which confers life through Christ?

*(Verse 18.) Therefore as by the offence of one judgment came upon all men to condemnation; even so by the righteousness of one the free gift came upon all men unto justification of life*: that is, as through the offence of one all who sinned in the same way deserved condemnation, so in the righteousness of one all who believe will be justified. If any think this condemnation is general, they will in the same way take the justification to be general as well. But that is not true, because not all believe.

*(Verse 19.) For as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous.* Those whom above he called "all", here he means as "the many": for many, not all, followed Adam's offence by transgressing; and many, not all, will be made righteous through faith in Christ. Death, then, did not reign over those who did not sin after the likeness of Adam's transgression.

*(Verse 20.) Moreover the law entered, that the offence might abound.* It can be said in reply: then the Law ought not to have been given, lest sins should increase; for if before the Law sin was less, there was no need of law. But the Law was plainly necessary, to show that sins which were believed to be committed with impunity are imputed before God; so that for the future men should know what they ought to avoid. Hence the prophet Isaiah says, *The law is given for a help* (Isaiah 8:20): so that, since the seeds of righteousness are in some way sown in nature itself, the Law was added, by whose authority and teaching the natural disposition might go forward to bearing the fruit of righteousness. For as what is born perishes unless it has nourishment by which it may be cherished and grow up, so the natural disposition to righteousness, unless it has something to look to and reverence, does not readily go forward, but sickens and yields to the sins that come upon it. For it is weighed down by the habit of offending, so that it does not grow into fruit, and so it is extinguished. The Law, then, was given with foresight, for a help, as the prophet testifies; but the people, following their old habit, multiplied their sins. For by the very practice they began to sin more than they had sinned before; and so it came about that, once the Law was given, sins were not lessened, but abounded. For the Apostle shows what came about when the Law was given, not what the Law did. But how did sins grow by a fault of the Law, which threatened lest men should sin, so that it is said to have entered that sin might abound? It is true that it entered as one that would help, and with lowliness; but afterwards it began to lord it over those whom it had commanded not to sin, and they sinned. For the law began to show the abundance of sins, when the things it had forbidden were done more than they had been done before. In this way, then, the law was given that the offence might abound. For the Apostle, commending faith, through which sins are blotted out, said that the law brought it about that sin abounded, as I said above: because it was not given that sins should be abolished but that they should abound, since it showed men to be sinners before the law, and after the law held them all guilty. *But where sin abounded, grace did much more abound.* It is plain that, when sin abounded, grace abounded much more: when God's gift came, according to the promise, and covered the sins of all; so that the devil's envy grieves that it has gained nothing. For when the law had been given for the good of men, the devil worked to overturn it by urging what was unlawful; so that what had been given to help should turn out the opposite, when the commandments were despised, so that what came from the Law was not benefit but judgment for punishment. Then God, just and merciful, to bring to nothing the glory which the devil had sought from his triumph over man, decreed that His Son should come, who would forgive all sins; so that the joy from the gift of grace should be greater than the grief from sin had been. For the joy of God's gift profits even those over whom Satan could not triumph; and therefore grace did much more abound over the sin to which Satan had urged men.

*(Verse 21.) That as sin hath reigned unto death, even so might grace reign through righteousness unto eternal life by Jesus Christ our Lord.* Sin reigned, seeing its work profit sinners unto death, in which it gloried; in the same way grace reigns through righteousness unto eternal life, through Jesus Christ our Lord: so that, as sin begun through Adam reigned, so grace reigns through Christ. And grace reigns through righteousness in this way: if, having received the forgiveness of sins, we follow righteousness; so that grace, seeing that it has fruit in the good whom it has redeemed, may reign unto eternal life, knowing that we shall be eternal. Grace, then, abounds more, because sin reigned for a time, but grace for ever. For when grace reigns, it is the kingdom of God, as, when sin reigned, it was the kingdom of the devil. Yet he refers it all to Christ, so that all the grace of God may be learned from Christ. He then answers the sense above, saying:
