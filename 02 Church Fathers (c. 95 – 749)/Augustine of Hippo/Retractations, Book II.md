# Retractations, Book II

*Book II covers the works written as a bishop, from the questions to Simplician (396) to the last before the review itself (c. 427). The translator numbers the chapters straight on from Book I.*

## Chapter XXVII. To Simplician, in Two Books

1. Of the books which I worked out as a bishop, the first two are addressed to Simplician, Bishop in the church at Milan, who succeeded the most beloved Ambrose; they have to do with different questions, two of which I brought together in the first book out of the epistle of Paul to the Romans.

2. The first of these (questions) is; "Concerning. what was written: ‘Therefore, what shall we say? Is the law sin? God forbid," up to that place where he says: Who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord.'" In that section, these words of the apostle: "'The law is spiritual, but I am carnal, " etc., in which the flesh is ‘shown to be in conflict against the spirit, I have set forth as though man is described as constituted until now "under the law," not yet "under grace;" for, a long time afterwards I learned: that those words could also be applied to the spiritual man—and this is more likely.

3. The second question in this book is from that passage where he says: "'Now, not only that, but also Rebecca having conceived from one coitus with our father Isaac, '" up to that hastens where he says: "'Unless the Lord of Sabaoth had left us a seed, we had been made like Sodom and been similar to Gomorrah." In the solution of this question, without doubt, I worked in behalf of the freedom of the will of man, but the grace of God conquered, end it could not have reached anything except that, as the apostle is understood to have said with very clear truth: "For who sets you apart? Now, what do you have which you have not received? But if you did receive it, why do you glory as if you had not received it? The martyr Cyprian, desiring also to indicate this whole matter, explained it under this same title, saying: "There is nothing of which to boast when nothing is ours,

4. In the second book other questions are discussed and are explained so far as our moderate ability allowed; these all have to do with that part of the Scripture which is called Kings. The first of these is "concerning what was written: 'And the spirit of the Lord leapt in on saul, since it was said at another place: "And an evil spirit of the Lord was in Saul, "

5. When I was explaining that I said: "Although what one wills is within his power, nevertheless, what he can do is not within his power, " This was said because we only say that is within our power which, when we will it, is done, where to will is first and foremost. To be sure, that will is present without any interval of time when we will, but this power we also receive from above for the purpose of living correctly, since "the will is prepared by the Lord,

6. The second question is: "In what manner it was said: 'I repent that I designated soul,

7. The third: "Whether the unclean spirit which was in the witch would have been able to bring it about that Samuel was seen by Saul and that he spoke with him, "

8. The fourth: "Concerning what was written: 'King David went in and set before the Lord.'"

9. The fifth: "Concerning what Elijah said: '0 Lord, a witness of this widow, with whom I dwell at her house, you have done ill to kill her son.'"

10. This work begins with these words: "Certainly very pleasant and very gratifying."

## Chapter XXVIII. Against the Epistle of Manichaeus Which They Call Fundamental, in One Book

1. The book against the epistle of manichaeus which they call fundamental refutes only the beginning of the letter; but to other parts of it notations have been attached where it seemed proper, by which the entire work is overthrown and which would serve as reminders to me, if the leisure time had ever come to write against the entire work.

2. This book begins in this manner: "The one true almighty God."

## Chapter XXIX. On Struggle, in One Book

1. A book was written on Christian Struggle in the form of a colloquial discourse to the brethren who were unlearned in Latin rhetoric; it contains a rule of faith and precepts for living.

2. In this book, the following passage was set forth: "Let us not listen to those who deny the future resurrection of the flesh and who remind us of what the apostle Paul says: 'Flesh and blood will not take possession of the kingdom of God, ' not understanding what that same apostle says: 'This corruptible must put on incorruption and this mor tal must put on immortality; " for when this will have been accomplished, it will not be flesh and blood any longer but a heavenly body. " This must not be understood as though the future substance will not be flesh, but the apostle is to be understood to have called by the name of flesh and blood that corruption of flesh and blood which will not exist st all in that kingdom where flesh will be incorruptible; but these words of the apostle can be understood in another way; that by flesh and blood he meant the works of flesh and blood, and that those who perseveringly cherished those works would not take possession of the kingdom of God.

3. This book begins with these words: "The crown of victory."

## Chapter XXX. On Christian Doctrine, in Four Books

1. When I discovered that the books on Christian doctrine were not finished I chose to finish them rather than to leave them as they were and pass on to the revision of others. Accordingly, I completed the third book, which had been written as far as the place where there is brought to mind a quotation from the gospel about the woman who "hid yeast in three measures of meal, until the whole amount was leavened." I also added the last book and completed the work in four books; the first three of them are of assistance in understanding the Scriptures; but the fourth shows the means of making known the things we have interpreted,

2. Now, in the second book, to be sure, concerning the author of the book which many call the Wisdom of Solomon I learned afterwards that it was not as well-established as I said it was that Jesus Sirach wrote this book as well as the book of Ecclesiasticus; and I found out that it was more probable that he was not the author of it at all.

3. Again, when I said: "The authority of the old Testament is contained within these forty-four books," I used the phrase "old Testament" in accordance with ecclesiastical usage; but the apostle seems to use the phrase "old Covenant," only of what was given "on fount Sinai.

4. And in the passage in which I said: "Saint Ambrose explained a difficulty relating to chronology, " as if Plato and Jeremiah were contemporaries, ny memory failed me. For what the bishop had said concerning this matter may be read in a book of his, which he wrote on sacraments or on philosophy.

5. This work begins in this way: "There are certain: rules."

## Chapter XXXI. Against the Sect of Donatus, in Two Books

1. There are two books of mine entitled Against the Sect of Done tus.

2. In the first I said: "I do not like having schismatics violently herded back to Catholic communion by the might of any secular authority." And I certainly did not like it then, because I had not yet realized either how much evil their hope of impunity would dare nor how much opportunity or changing for the better careful discipline might present to them.

3. This work begins with these words: "Since the Donatists . . . to us."

## Chapter XXXII. Of Confessions, in Thirteen Books

1. The thirteen books of my Confessions praise the Just and good God, both in the good things and in the bad things, and they awaken the human intellect and heart to him; however, what is of consequence to me is that they stirred this up in me when they were written, and they are Stirring it now when they are read. What others may feel about them, they themselves have seen; but I know that they have given much pleasure to many of the brethren and are giving pleasure to them. From the first to the tenth book, they were written concerning myself; in the other three, concerning the sacred scriptures, from what was written: "In the beginning God made the heaven and the earth, right up to the rest of the Sabbath,

2. In the fourth book, when I acknowledged the affliction of my soul at the death of a friend, saying that our souls had been made one out of two in some way: "And, therefore," I said, "perchance, I was afraid to die, lest he whom I had loved greatly should die wholly;" this seems tO me as if it is a light declamation rather than a serious confession, although this absurdity is moderated somehow by that "Perchance" which was added.

3. And in the thirteenth book I said this: "The firmament" was made "between the spiritual upper waters and the corporeal lower waters;" it was said in no sufficiently deliberate manner; but the matter is very much in obscurity.

4. This work begins with these words: "You are great, O Lord."

## Chapter XXXIII. Against Faustus the Manichaean, in Thirty-three Books

1. Against Faustus the Manichaean, blasphemer of the law and the prophets and their lord and the incarnation of Christ, saying that the scriptures of the New Testament, in which it is clearly shown, are falsified, I wrote a large work giving my replies to his main points. There are thirty-three discussions. Why did I not speak of them as books? For although there are some among them that are short, they are, nevertheless, books. In fact, one of them, in which the life of the patriarchs is defended by us in opposition to his accusations, is of such size that it can be compared with scarcely any of my other books.

2. Accordingly, in the third book when I explain the question how Joseph could have two fathers, I certainly said: "Because he was begotten of one, and adopted by the other;"3 but I ought also to have mentioned the kind of adoption; for what I said sounds as if another living father adopted him. Now the law relating to death also referred to adopting sons, ordering that, after the death of the brother without sons, "the brother marry the wife and raise offspring from her to his dead brother." This explanation really answers with less confusion the question concerning two fathers of one man. Now the brothers were born of the same mother, about which this case had to do; as the one who was called Eli died, the other married his wife; this latter was Jacob, by whom, according to the narrative of Matthew, Joseph was begotten; but he (Jacob) was begotten for his uterine brother, whose son Luke says Joseph was; Joseph was not begotten at all by Jacob, but legally adopted. This was discovered among the writings of those who had written concerning this circumstance immediately after the ascension of our Lord. For Julius Africanus gives even the name of this same woman who bore Jacob, the father of Joseph by her first husband Mathan, who was the father of Jacob and the grandfather of Joseph, according to Matthew; and who by her second husband Melchi bore Heli, of whom Joseph was the adopted son. I had certainly not yet read this when I answered Faustus, but, nevertheless, I could not doubt that it was possible for it to happen by means of adoption that one man might have two fathers,

3. In the twelfth and thirteenth books it was argued concerning the second son of Noah, who is called Ham, that Canaan was cursed by Noah, as though Ham had been cursed by his father in his own person, and not in his son canaan, as the Scripture shows.

4. In the fourteenth book it was said concerning the sun and the moon that they hear and consequently sustain the ir misguided worshipers, although these words could be understood as transferring the manner of speaking from the animate level to the inanimate, which is called a metaphor by the Greeks, as it was written concerning the sea, that "it rages in the womb of its mother wishing to came forth, when it certainly does not possess desire.

5. In the twenty-ninth book I wrote: "It should be denied that there is also anything shameful in the genitals of the saints. To be sure, some are considered repulsive, because they do not have the same appearance of comeliness as those parts of the body which are left uncovered; but there is a more plausible reason, which has been stated in our writings afterwards, why the apostle also called it repulsive, " that is, "because of the law in my bodily parts contending against the law of my mind, " which comes to pass because of sin, not because of the original disposition of our nature.

6. This work begins in this way: "A certain Faustus was, "

## Chapter XXXIV. Against Felix the Manichaean, in Two Books

1. For two days I argued in the church in the presence of the people against a certain Manichaean, Felix by name. For he had come to Hippo to plant that error; for he was one of their doctors; although unlearned in liberal scholarship, he was, nevertheless, more clever than Fortunatus. These proceedings are official documents of the church, but they are considered among my books. So, there are two books, in the second of which there is a treatment of the freedom of the will, whether for performing the evil or the good. But concerning the grace in which they truly become free—of whom this was written: "If the son shall have made you free, you will then indeed be free" —ye were forced by no necessity to investigate it more assiduously, of Fe since he was the kind of opponent with whom we had to 40.

2. This work begins with these words: "In the sixth consulate of Honorius Augustus, on the seventh of December."

## Chapter XXXV. On the Nature of the Good, in One Book

1. This is a book written in opposition to the Manichaeans on the nature of the good. In this book it is indicated that God is unchangeable nature and the highest good, and that from him there are other natures, whether spiritual or physical, and that all of them, in so far as they are natures, are good, and what or whence is evil and how much evil the Manichaeans put into the nature of the good and how.much good into the nature of the evil, and what natures their heresy devises.,

2. This book begins with these words: "The highest good is God, than whom there is no higher.”

## Chapter XXXVI. Against Secundinus, the Manichaean, in One Book

1. A certain Secundinus, who was not one of those whom the manichaeans call the elect, but one of the auditors, with whom I had not become acquainted at all by sight, wrote to me as if he were a friend, respectfully remonstrating with me for attacking that heresy in my writings, and advising me not to do it, and encouraging me rather to accompany it with its defense and the reproof of the Catholic faith. I replied to him; but because I did not put at the beginning of this work who was writing to whom, it was kept not among my letters but among my books. His letter was also written down there from the beginning. Now the: title of this volume of mine is: Against Secundinus, the Manichaean. According to my way of thinking I place this first of all which I have been able to write against that curse.

2. This book begins with these words: "The good will toward me which appears in your writings."

## Chapter XXXVII. Against Hilarus, in One Book

1. In the midst of these duties a certain Hilarus, a man who had been a tribune, a Catholic layman, provoked—I don't know why he kept railing against the ministers of God with abusive reprimands wherever he could, as was his habit—a custom which had then arisen at Carthage, saying it ought not to be followed—the custom, that is, of saying hymns from the Book of Psalms, either before the oblation or while what had been offered was being disturbed to the people. Having been requested by the brethren, I replied to this attack and the book was called:.gainst Hilarus.

2. This book begins thus: "They who make mention of the old Testament."

## Chapter XXXVIII. Questions on the Gospels, in Two Books

1. There are some explanations of a few places out of the gospel according to Matthew and others in like manner from the gospel according to Luke; in the first book those from matthew are collected and in the other those from Luke. The title of this work is: Questions On the Gospels. But why only those passages that are explained were taken from those gospels mentioned above—those passages which are included in these books——and what they are, my preface satisfactorily points out, by joining and considering those questions in such a way that anyone who wants to read may ascertain the parts in consecutive order.

2. Accordingly, in the first book, in this passage which was set down: "The Lord had repeated his passion to the two disciples apart, " the faultiness of the manuscript deceived us; for "twelve" was written, not "two."

3. In the second book, desiring to explain how Joseph could have had two fathers, the Joseph whose consort was named the virgin Mary, I said the following, because it is asserted that the brother had married the wife of his deceased brother, so that the brother would raise up seed for his dead brother, according to the law; "It is weak, because the law ordered the one who was born to take the name of the deceased. This is not true; for the name of the dead brother, which was described, the law enjoins to De applicable to the living one, so that the latter may be called his son, not in order that the son would be called by his same name.

4. This work begins with these words: "This work was not so written.":

## Chapter XXXIX. Observations on Job, in One Book

1. Whether the book, the title of which is: Observations On Job, may be considered mine or theirs who collected them, as they were able or wanted to, into one body of writing, transcribing them from the margins or the manuscript, I could not easily say. For they are pleasing to those who understand a little; such, however, are necessarily offended by the many unintelligible notes because the words themselves which are explained are not written down so as to make it clear what is being explained; then, so much uncertainty re-; suited from the brevity of the sayings that the reader could scarcely tolerate it; he had to pass over many things he did not understand; finally I learned that the work was so full of errors in our manuscripts that I could not correct it, nor would I want it said that it was published by ne, except I Know that the brethren have what by affection could not be refused.

2. This book begins in this way: "And to him there were great works on the earth.”

## Chapter XL. On the Instruction of Candidates for the Catechumenate, in One Book

1. There is also one book on the instruction of candidates for the catechumenate, a book which is designated by this very title. In this book, when I said: "Not even did the angel, who, with other spirits, his accomplices, gave up his obedience to God through pride and became the devil, do any harm to God, but to himself; for God knows how to set in order those souls who forsake him, " it would be more consistent to say: "those spirits who forsake nim, * for it was a question of angels.

2. This book begins in this way: “You have asked me, brother Deogratias."?’

## Chapter XLI. On the Trinity, in Fifteen Books

1. I wrote fifteen books concerning the Trinity, which is God, over a period of some years. But when I had not finished twelve of those books and I had held some who ardently wished to have them back longer than they could endure, they were taken away from me, less free from faults than they ought and could have been at the time that I had intended to publish them. I found that out later, because other copies of those books had remained in our possession; I had decided that I would not publish them at this tine, but have it in mind to say in some other work of mine what had happened to me in that regard. However, after I was urged by the brethren—be cause I did not have the strength to resist them—1 corrected as much as I considered ought to be corrected, and completed and published them, adding to them at the beginning a letter, which I wrote to the venerable Aurelius, bishop of the Carthaginian chur ch, in which I set forth, just as if it was a prologue, what had taken place, what I had intended to do as a result of my own reflection, and what I had done because of the constraining love of the brethren.

2. In the eleventh book of this treatise, when I was treating of the visible body, I said: "For this reason, to love this is to be estranged. " This was said concerning the love whereby something is loved in such a way that the one who loves thinks that he is blessed in the process of deriving enjoyment from it; for to love the bodily form in praise of the creator is not to be estranged, so that anyone enjoying the creator himself may be truly blessed.

3. And also in that same book, when I said: "and I "do not remember a bird with four feet, because I have not seen one, but, nevertheless, I contemplate the phantasy very easily, as long as I add to a certain winged from of the kind I have seen, two other feet, such as I have seen in the same way 3 in saying it I had not been able to recollect the winged quadrupeds of which the law makes mention. For it does not count as feet the two hindmost legs, with which the locusts jump, which it says are clean, and therefore it distinguishes them from such unclean winged creatures which do not jump with two legs, just as scarabs. As one might expect, all of this kind are called winged quadrupeds in the law.

4. In the twelfth book there is a passage which is just like an explanation of the words of the apostle, where he says: "Every sin whatsoever, which a man does, is outside the body; what was said does not give me satisfaction and I do not think that this statement—"but whoever commits fornication sins against his own body" —ought to have been understood as it was, as if he who does something does it for the sake of obtaining those things which are experienced by the body, in order that he may fix the end of his being in them. For this includes very many more sins other than that fornication which is effected in illicit concubinage, about which the apostle appears to have spoken when he said this.

5. This work, not including the letter, which was added a little later at the beginning of it, begins with these words: "He who is about to read what we have written about the trinity."

## Chapter XLII. On the Harmony of the Gospels, in Four Books

1. During those same years, in which I was little by little dictating the books on the trinity, I also wrote others by uninterrupted labor, interspersing them in the intervals, among which there are four books on the harmony of the gospels, on account of those who misrepresent them as contradicting each other. The first of these books was written against those who honor Christ as being especially wise, or suppose they honor him, and do not wish to believe the gospel, because they suppose that those things were not written by him, but by his disciples, who had mistakenly attributed divinity to him—the divinity with which God is invested.

2. In this book, there is, indeed, this statement: "The Hebrew race began with abraham," and this is credible— that the "Hebrei" seem to have been called the "Abrahei," but they are perceived to have been called more properly after him who was named Heber, as if they were "Heberei;" concerning this matter I have discoursed satisfactorily in the six-On the City of God.

3. In the second book, when I was treating of the two fathers of Joseph, I said that he was begotten of one and adopted by the other; but this should have been said: he was adopted unto the other. For it is more reasonable to say that he was adopted by the dead brother, according to the law, for the one who begot him took the wife of the one who had died.

4. Likewise, where I said: "In fact, Luke reaches the same David through Nathan, by which prophet God made amends for his sin, " I ought to have said "by a prophet of that name," lest he be regarded to have been the same: man as the other was, although he also was called by the same name.

5. This work begins in this manner: "Among all divine authorities,"

## Chapter XLIII. Against the Epistle of Parmenian, in Three Books

1. In certain books against the epistle of Parmenian, —bishop of the Donatists at Carthage and successor of Donatus, a new question is reflected upon and explained, whether in unity and in the fellowship of their sacraments the evil contaminate the good and in what way they do not contaminate them; this is discussed for the sake of the church extended throughout the whole world; to bring calumny on the world, they create a schism.

2. In the third book, when there is a discussion of the way in which what the apostle says must be understood: "Remove the wicked one from yourselves, I said this: "In order that anyone may remove the evil thing from himself. This is not to be understood in that way, but preferably in this way: that a bad man may be removed from among good men, which is done through ecclesiastical discipline, just as the Greek tongue reveals, where it was written without ambiguity, So that it may be understood to be: the evil man," not "the evil thing," although I replied to Parmenian in accordance with that first judgment.

3. This work begins with these words: "Certain men many things against other Donatists.”:

## Chapter XLIV. On Baptism, in Seven Books

1. I wrote seven books on baptism against the Donatists, who were attempting to protect themselves by the authority of the most blessed bishop and martyr Cyprian. In these books I taught that there was nothing so adequate for confuting the Donatists and for so entirely closing their mouths, that they could not defend their schism in opposition to the Catholic Church, as the writings and conduct of Cyprian.

2. Now wherever in these books I made mention of the "church not having stain or wrinkle, " I did not mean the church as it now is, but that institution which is being prepared in order that it may be glorious when it will appear. For now, because of certain ignorances and weaknesses of her members, daily the whole church needs to say: "Forgive us our debts.

3. In the place where I said: "(One) could have martyrdom in the place of baptism, " it was not just proper for me to offer as an example that thief, because it is doubtful whether or not he was baptized.

4. In the seventh book I followed the meaning of Cyprian relative to gold and silver vessels placed in a great house, which he took to mean the good ones, 8 and those of wood and clay as bad, referring to the former these words: "In fact, some are in honor," out to the latter these words: "Some certainly are in dishonor." With more reason I approve what I found and gave heed to in the writings of Tychonius; we should understand that in both, not only among the gold and silver, there are some which are in honor; and again in both not only among the wood and clay, some are in dishonor.

5. This work begins in this manner: "In those books which (we wrote) in opposition to the epistle of Parmenian."

## Chapter XLV. Against What Centurius Brought Over from the Donatists, in One Book

1. When we were treating of many things against the sect of Donatus in numerous discussions, a certain layman, at that time one of them, brought to the church some of what was said or written against us in brief testimonies, which they thought were favorable to their cause. I replied to them in a few words. The title of this pamphlet is: Against What Centurius Brought Over From the Donatists, and it begins thus: "In that place you are saying what was written in Solomon: 'To abstain from strange water.

## Chapter XLVI. On the Questions of Januarius, in Two Books

1. Two books, the title of which is; "On the Questions of Januarius," contain many arguments about the sacraments, which the church preserves either altogether or in part, i.e., not uniformly in all places; although I wasn't able to remember everything, nevertheless, I answered the inquiries well enough. The first of these books is a letter— since it has at the beginning the name of him to whom it is written—but, this work is counted among the books, because i the second book, which does not possess my name, is much more lengthy and many more things are discussed in it.

2. Now, in the first book, I said this concerning manna: "That it had a flavor in the mouth of each person as he wished." It did not occur to me where it could be proved, unless from the Book of Wisdom, which the Jews do not admit into their authoritative canon. Nevertheless, it could happen in the faithful, not in those who grumbled against God; the former certainly did not call for other food, if this manna tasted to them as they wanted it to.

3. This work begins with these words: "To those things which you have asked me,"

## Chapter XLVII. On the Work of Monks, in One Book

1. I was forced to write a book on the work of monks by the following necessity: when monasteries had begun to exist at Carthage, some maintained themselves with their hands, complying with the apos tle 3 others, however, wanted to live from the oblations of the faithful in such a way that, doing nothing from which they might possess or supply their necessities, they considered and boasted that they fulfilled better that apostolic precept, in which the Lord says: "Consider the birds of the heaven and the lilies of the field, Whence, also, among laymen of an inferior purpose, but, nevertheless, burning with zeal, there had begun to exist turbulent dissensions, by which the church was agitated, some defending one side, some the other. To this was added the circumstance that some of them, who said that they would not work, were long-haired, Whence the contentions on the one side of those who quarreled, on the other of those who were almost battling, increased according to the exertions of the parties. On account of this, the venerable old Aurelius, bishop of that same city, entreated me to write something on this cause, and I did.

2. This book begins in this manner: "it your entreaty, holy brother Aurelius."

## Chapter XLVIII. On the Good of Marriage, in One Book

1. The heresy of Jovinian, having placed the worth of holy virgins on an equality with the virtue of marriage, has so much influence in the city of Rome that some even of the holy women, of whose impurity there had been no suspicion, had thrown themselves into marriage, it was said, principally with this justification, since he had urged them, saying: "Are you, therefore, better than sarah, better than Susannah or Anna?" and other commendable women, of whom the scripture testimony reminds us, than whom they cannot think themselves better nor even equal. In this manner, he also broke up the sacred celibacy of holy men by mentioning and comparing the marriages of the fathers. The holy church, which is located there, resists that heretic very faithfully and very powerfully. But these arguments of his had continued in certain rumors and whisperings, which none dared to urge openly. But even this secretly creeping poison had to be resisted with the power which the Lord gives, especially since it was intimated that Jovinian could not be answered with the praise, but with the censure of marriage. Because of this I published a book, the title of which is: On the Good of Marriage. In it, is the great question of the procreation of children, before men merited death through sin, since the subject seems to be the sexual intercourse of mortal bodies; but, later, in some of our other writings, this is explained in what I consider to be a satisfactory manner.

2. I also said, in a certain place: "For what food is for the health of the body, this sexual intercourse is for the health of the race, and each is not without carnal pleasure, which, if it is regulated and forced back into natural use by a restraining self-control, cannot be lust. " This was said because the good and right use of lust is not lust. For just as it is evil to use good things wrongly, so it is good to use bad things well; concerning this matter I have composed other works more assiduously, especially those against those new heretics, the Pelagians.

3. I said this concerning Abraham: "Out of this obedience, that father, Abraham, who was not without a wife, was prepared to be without an only son, who was to be slain by himself; I do not fully approve of that statement. For, we should rather believe that abraham believed that, if his son were killed, he would be restored to him soon by resuscitation, 9 just as it is read in the letter which is to the Hebrews.

4. This book begins in this manner; "Whereas each individual men is a part of the human race."

## Chapter XLIX. On Holy Virginity, in One Book

1. After I wrote On the Good of Marriage, it was expected that I would write concerning holy virginity; I did not delay to point out, as much as I could in one volume, that it (holy virginity) is the gift of God, and how great a gift it is, and with how much humility it had to be defended.

2. This book begins in this manner: "We have recently published a book on the good of marriage."

## Chapter L. On Genesis, According to the Letter, in Twelve Books

1. ## Chapter LXIX. On the City of God, in Twenty-two Books

1. In the meantime, Rome, in en invasion of the Goths, advancing under their king, Alaric, was over thrown by their disastrous attack. The worshippers of false gods and many gods, to whom we colloquially refer as pagans, tried to blame this on the Christian religion and began to blaspheme the true God with more bitterness and more severity than usual. Thereupon inflamed "by the zeal of the house of God, "9 I began to write, against their blasphemies and errors, the books on the city of God. This work kept me occupied for several years, because many other things intervened which could not be put off and which had to be completed first. But this great work on the city of God was completed, nevertheless, in twenty-two books. The first five of them refute those who wish human affairs to be so prosperous that they think the veneration of many gods, which the pagans were wont to revere, is necessary for this; and maintain that it is because they are forbidden, that these evils arise and abound. Now, the following five are addressed to those who acknowledge that these evils have never been wanting and never will be among mortals and that these evils, now great, now small, vary in places, times, and persons, but who insist that the veneration of many gods, in which sacrifice is offered to them, will be useful for the life after death. accordingly, in these ten books, these two groundless opinions, contrary to the Christian religion, are refuted.

2. But, that no one might reprove us for having refuted other ideas at such length and not having asserted our own, the other part of this work, which consists of twelve books, has to do with the latter object; although, when there is need, we have also advanced opinions of our own in the first two books, and we have refuted contrary opinions in the last twelve. Accordingly, the first four of the last twelve books contain the origin of the two cities, one of which is of God, the other of this world; the second four give their extension, or projection; but the third, also the last, gives their appropriate end. Thus, since all twenty-two books were composed concerning each city, yet they have received a title from the better city, so that they are called, preferably, On the City of God.

## Chapter LXXX. Of Discourses, in Seven Books

1. I produced seven books concerning seven books of the Holy Scriptures, i.e., five of moses, one of Joshua the son of Nun, and the other of Judges, marking the phraseology of the several Scripture passages where it is at variance with our usage, and which they who read scrutinize with too little attention to the meaning, when we have an idiom. Sometimes they elicit something which is certainly not repugnant to the truth; however, the author, by whom it was written, is found not to have meant just that, but appears more likely to have used an idiom. Now, many things which are unintelligible in the Holy Scriptures grow light when the idiomatic expression is recognized. Because of this, those kinds of discourse are to be discerned in which the meaning is clear, so that even where the meaning is not clear, this same discernment may help and may show to the attention inherent meanings. The title of this work is: Discourses on Genesis, and in the same way on the several books.

2. Now, in the first book, I quoted this passage of Scripture: "ind noah performed all the words which the Lord directed him, thus he aid," and I said that that utterance: was similar to the one which was spoken on the nature of created things; after "and so it was done was said, "and God made" ” was added, which seems to me in no way similar to the first statement. In the former the meaning is latent; in the latter we have only a manner oi speaking.

3. This work begins in this manner: "The discourses of the Scriptures"

## Chapter LXXXI. Of Questions, in Seven Books

We had also begun to inspect a long time ago the books of the Kings in that way, but, not making much progress on the others which were more urgent, we gave attention to the spirit.

Now, in the first book, in which there is a representation of the variegated rods which Jacob placed in the: water so that in conception the sheep might see them when they drank and give birth to variegated offspring, I did not well explain the reason why he did not place them, again, before those who conceived, i.e., when they begot other offspring, but only at the first conception. For the explanation of the other question, in which it is asked why Jacob said to his father-in-law: "You have eluded my wages by ten ewe lambs, "4 which was quite true, shows that this one was not properly solved.

3. In the third book, also, where there is a consideration of the high priest, how he could become a father when it was necessary that he enter into the holy of holies, where the altar of incense was, for the offering of the incense in the morning and in the evening, into which he cannot enter unclean, as the law says, and the ‘same law says that man is Pp. defiled by conjugal intercourse, whom it orders to be washed: in water, but it says that even after one has washed, he is "unclean until the evening hence I said: "It necessarily follows either that he remain continent, or that the incense be neglected for several days"—I did not perceive that it was not strictly necessary. For what was written: "He will be unclean until the evening,” can be understood in this way: that he was not unclean all through the evening, but until the evening, so that he, being pure, may offer incense now at evening time, since, for the purpose of begetting sons, he could cohabit with his wife after the early morning incense had been mixed.

## Chapter LXXXII. On the Soul and Its Origin, in Four Books

1. At that time a certain Vincent Victor in Mauretania Caesariensis discovered one little work of mine at the house Of a certain presbyter, Peter the spaniard; in this treatise, in a certain passage on the origin of the souls of individual men, I had confessed that I did not know whether they are propagated from the soul of the first man and thereafter from parents or Whether they are assigned to individuals severally without propagation just as to the first one, but that I certainly do know that the soul is not body, but spirit; and in opposition to these ideas of mine, he wrote to this Peter two books, which the monk Renatus sent me from Caesarea. Having read these books, I made my reply in four others: one addressed to the monk Renatus, another to the presbyter Peter, and two to Victor himself. Put the one to Peter has the diffuse book; however, it is a letter, which I did not wish to consider separately from the other three. Gaudentius replied by writing two letters, one short and hastily prepared, since the post-bearer was in haste, the other a lengthy one as if he were replying more completely and more earnestly. When this work came into the hands of Gaudentius, he wrote back that Ho had seen it, making no reasoned reply to myself, but rather showing that he could neither answer nor keep silent.
