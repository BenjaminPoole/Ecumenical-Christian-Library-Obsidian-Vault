# VIII.5. To call and dissolve all solemn assemblies about the public affairs of the Church.

V. [^1]The consuls of Rome Polybius affirmeth[^2] to have had a kind of regal authority, in that they might call together the senate and people whensoever it pleased them. Seeing therefore the affairs of the Church and Christian religion are public affairs, for the ordering whereof more solemn assemblies sometimes are of as great importance and use, as they are for secular affairs; it seemeth no less an act of supreme authority to call the one than the other. Wherefore amongst sundry other[^3] prerogatives of Simon’s dominion over the Jews, this[^4] is reckoned as[^5] not the least, “that no man might gather any great assembly in the land without him.” For so the manner of Jewish regiment had always been, that whether the cause for which men assembled themselves in peaceable, good, and orderly course[^6], were ecclesiastical or civil, supreme authority should assemble them. David gathered all Israel together unto Jerusalem, when the ark was to be removed; he assembled the sons of Aaron and the Levites[^7]. Solomon did the like at such time as the temple was to be dedicated[^8]: when the Church was to be reformed, Asa in his time did the same: the same upon like occasions done afterwards by Joas, Ezekias, Josias, and others[^9].

[^10]The ancient imperial law[^11] forbiddeth such assemblies as the emperor’s authority did not cause to be made. Before emperors became Christian[^12], the Church had never any synod general[^13]; their greatest meetings[^14] consisted[^15] of bishops and others the gravest in each province. As for the civil governor’s authority, it suffered them only as things not regarded or accounted[^16] of, at such times as it did suffer them. So that what right a Christian king hath as touching assemblies of that kind we are not able to judge, till we come unto later times, when religion had won the hearts of the highest powers. Constantine (as Pighius[^17] doth grant) was not only the first that ever did call any general council together, but even the first that devised the calling of them for consultation about the business[^18] of God. After he had once given the example, his successors[^19] a long time followed the same; insomuch that S. Jerome, to disprove the authority of a synod which was pretended to be general, useth[^20] this as a forcible argument[^21], “Dic quis imperator hanc synodum jusserit convocari.” Their answer hereunto is no answer, which say, “that [^22]emperors did not this without conference had with [^23]bishops:” for to our purpose it is enough, if the clergy alone did it not otherwise than by the leave or[^24] appointment of their sovereign lords and kings.

Whereas therefore it is on the contrary side alleged, that Valentinian the elder[^25] being requested by Catholic bishops to grant that there might be a synod for the ordering of matters called in question by the Arians, answered, that he being one of the laity might not meddle with such affairs[^26], and thereupon wished[^27], that the priests and bishops, to whom the care of those things belonged[^28], should meet and consult thereof[^29] by themselves wheresoever[^30] they thought good: we must together[^31] with the emperor’s speech weigh the occasion and the[^32] drift thereof. Valentinian and Valens, the one a Catholic, the other an Arian, were emperors together: Valens the governor of the east, Valentinian[^33] of the west empire. Valentinian therefore taking his journey from the east part into the west[^34], and passing for[^35] that intent through Thracia, the bishops there[^36] which held the soundness of Christian belief, because they knew that Valens was their professed enemy, and therefore it the other were[^37] once departed out of those quarters, the Catholic cause was like to find small[^38] favour, moved presently Valentinian about a council to be assembled under the countenance of his authority; who by likelihood considering what inconvenience might thereby grow[^39], inasmuch as it could not be but a mean[^40] to incense Valens the more against them, refused himself to be author of, or present at any such assembly; and of this his denial gave them a colourable reason, to wit, that he was although an emperor, yet a secular person, and therefore not able in matters of so great obscurity to sit as a competent judge; but, if they which were bishops and learned men did think good to consult thereof together, they might. Whereupon when they could not obtain that which they most desired, yet that which was[^41] granted them[^42] they took, and forthwith had a council. Valentinian went on towards Rome, they remaining in consultation till Valens which accompanied him returned back; so that now there was no remedy, but either to incur a manifest contempt, or else at the hands even[^43] of Valens himself to seek approbation of that they had done. To him, therefore, they became suitors: his answer was short, “Either Arianism, or else[^44] exile, which they would;” whereupon their banishment ensued. Let reasonable men therefore now[^45] be judges, how much this example of Valentinian doth make against the authority, which we say that sovereign rulers may lawfully have as concerning synods and meetings ecclesiastical.

[^46]The clergy, in such wise gathered together, is an ecclesiastical senate, which with us, as in former times the chiefest prelate at his discretion did use to assemble, so[^47] afterwards in such considerations as have been before specified, it seemed[^48] more meet to annex the said prerogative unto the crown. The plot of reformed discipline not liking hereof[^49] so well, taketh order that every former assembly before it break[^50] up should itself appoint both the time and place of their after meeting again. But because I find not any thing on that side particularly alleged against us herein, a longer disputation about so plain a cause shall not need.
